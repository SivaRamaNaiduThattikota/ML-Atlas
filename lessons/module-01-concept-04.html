01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Overfitting memorizes the sample; underfitting can't even describe it

Overfitting is what happens when a model is flexible enough to fit the training sample's noise, not just the pattern behind it — training loss keeps falling, sometimes to nearly zero, while true performance stalls or gets worse. Underfitting is the opposite failure: the model is too simple, too rigid, to capture the real pattern even in the data it was trained on — so training loss and true performance stay high, together, because neither number was ever going to be good.

Concept 03's generalization gap is the symptom that something is wrong. Overfitting and underfitting are the two different problems that produce it — and once you check both numbers side by side, they look almost nothing alike.

First memory: overfitting looks like low training error, high true error. Underfitting looks like both errors staying high.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Every model has to choose how much room it's given

The hypothesis class a model is drawn from — the whole set of shapes it's even capable of producing — sets a ceiling and a floor at the same time. Make that class too rigid, and no amount of training data helps: it simply cannot express the real pattern, so underfitting is decided before training even starts. Make that class too flexible relative to a finite training sample, and it has enough freedom to trace the sample's specific noise as if it were signal — overfitting is Concept 01's empirical risk minimization doing exactly what it was asked to do, given too much room to work with.

TOO RIGID

A hypothesis class that can't express the true pattern at all, no matter how it's trained.

TOO FLEXIBLE

A hypothesis class with enough freedom to fit the sample's noise as if it mattered.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Same four points, three different curves through them

Picture four historical complaints, each plotted by some numeric feature on the x-axis against a relief outcome on the y-axis, with a real underlying pattern connecting them. Fit a flat, constant prediction to all four and you get a single horizontal line that ignores the pattern entirely — close to none of the four points, because it never tried to follow any of them. Fit a smooth, gently-sloped curve and it lands close to every point without touching any exactly, tracking the shape rather than the specific values. Fit an aggressively flexible curve — one with enough twists on offer — and it can be forced to pass through every one of the four points exactly, at the cost of wild swings anywhere between and beyond them.

FLAT LINE

Same prediction everywhere — misses the pattern on every training point.

SMOOTH CURVE

Close to every point, exact on none, roughly on-pattern just beyond them too.

WILD CURVE

Exact on every training point, unrecognizable anywhere else.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Two numbers, checked together, tell the two failures apart

UNDERFITTING

Both errors stay high

Training error is high and true error is high too — the two numbers are close to each other, and both are bad.

OVERFITTING

Training error looks great; true error doesn't

Training error is near zero while true error stays high — a large gap between two very different numbers.

A REASONABLE FIT

Both errors are low, and close

Training error and true error are both small and close to each other — the target this whole course is aiming at.

HYPOTHESIS CLASS

The set of shapes a model can even try

Underfitting and overfitting describe a fitted model relative to this class and the sample size — not in absolute terms.

CAPACITY IS RELATIVE

Same model, opposite failure on a different sample

A model that overfits four points can underfit four thousand — capacity only means anything relative to how much data it faces.

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

Capacity is a dial, not a switch

CAPACITY TOO LOW

The hypothesis class can't represent the real pattern — underfitting, regardless of how training goes.

CAPACITY TOO HIGH FOR THE SAMPLE

The hypothesis class can absorb the sample's noise — overfitting, once training minimizes empirical risk far enough.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often misunderstand

"High training error always means underfitting."

It could also mean a bug, a broken feature, or the wrong loss function — underfitting is one possible cause, not automatic.

"A more complex model is always the safer choice."

More capacity trades safety from underfitting for risk of overfitting — it's a trade, not a free upgrade.

"If training error is basically zero, the model is doing great."

That is exactly the profile to go check for overfitting, not to celebrate on its own.

"Overfitting only happens with huge or deep models."

A four-parameter curve fit to four points overfits just as surely as a large neural network — Section 10 shows the numbers.

"Underfitting means the model needs more data."

More data helps a model flexible enough to use it. It does nothing for a model whose hypothesis class can't express the pattern at all.

"You can tell which failure is happening from training error alone."

Both failures require training error and true or held-out error checked together — one number alone can't distinguish them.

04
INTUITION

The one-size shirt versus the one-fitting shirt

A one-size shirt is cut from a single fixed pattern regardless of who wears it — comfortable for almost no one, because it never had the flexibility to adapt to real variation in the first place. That is underfitting: too rigid to capture the pattern it's facing, no matter how carefully it's made. A bespoke shirt tailored to the exact way one customer happened to be standing, breathing, and slouching during a single fitting session — including a slouch that isn't even how they normally stand — fits that one session perfectly and pinches uncomfortably every time after. That is overfitting: fit to incidental detail of one specific sample, not the durable shape underneath it.

Analogy boundaryA shirt pattern isn't a hypothesis class, and a fitting session isn't a training sample. The analogy only conveys "too rigid to adapt" versus "fit to incidental detail" — not the actual mechanics of empirical risk minimization.

06
MECHANISM

How a hypothesis class produces either failure, traced

Choose a hypothesis class of some fixed flexibility for the learning algorithm to search
Concept 01's empirical risk minimization fits the best f it can find inside that class to the training sample
If the class can't express the true pattern, no f inside it gets close on training data either — R̂ₙ(f) stays high
If the class is flexible enough to also fit the sample's specific noise, R̂ₙ(f) can be driven near zero
That low R̂ₙ(f) reflects the noise the class latched onto, not the pattern, so R(f) does not fall the same way — the gap opens
Checking both numbers together, never either alone, is what tells the two failures apart

Every later capacity-control tool in this course — regularization, tree-depth limits, early stopping, cross-validated model selection — exists to steer this same branching test toward the middle case, not to eliminate the branching itself.

09
MATHEMATICS

Characterizing both failures without yet decomposing them

SymbolMeaning
R(f), R̂ₙ(f)True risk and empirical risk, as defined in Concept 03
R*The best true risk any model could in principle reach on this problem — informal here, including irreducible noise and the best-case fit
gap(f)R(f) − R̂ₙ(f), Concept 03's generalization gap
underfit(f): R̂ₙ(f) ≈ R(f) and both ≫ R*
overfit(f): gap(f) = R(f) − R̂ₙ(f) is large, with R̂ₙ(f) ≈ R* or lower

These two inequalities describe the shape of each failure without deriving why either one happens for a given model and sample size — that derivation is Concept 05's job. There, R(f) is decomposed into a bias term (systematic error from a hypothesis class too rigid to reach R*) and a variance term (sensitivity to exactly which sample was drawn), and R* itself is defined precisely as the irreducible error plus the best any hypothesis class could achieve. Neither decomposition is derived here; this section only gives the diagnostic inequalities a name.

EXAMPLE

Three curves, hand-computed, through the same four points

A population of six records, x = 1 through 6, following the true rule y = 2x, giving y = [2,4,6,8,10,12]. Training sample: the first four, with one point deliberately noisy — x_train = [1,2,3,4], y_train = [2,4,6,9] (the true value at x=4 is 8; the training set records 9). Held-out check: x_test = [5,6], y_test = [10,12], untouched by any of the three fits below.

Underfit — constantŷ = 5.25 (the training mean) for every x6.687534.0625Both errors high — can't even describe the training rows
A reasonable fit — linearŷ = 2.3x − 0.5, the least-squares line through the four points0.0751.345Both errors low and close — recovers the true rule despite the noisy point

The constant model's training MSE: predictions [5.25,5.25,5.25,5.25] against [2,4,6,9] give squared errors 10.5625, 1.5625, 1.5625, 14.0625 — sum 27.75, mean 6.6875. Section 10's exact case, generalized: raise the degree far enough past what the data supports and true error gets worse, not better.

BOUNDARY CASE

A small, genuinely closed population

If a population truly never produces anything beyond what was already sampled, even an aggressively fit model can coincidentally hold up — rare, and risky to assume without evidence.

COUNTEREXAMPLE

A deliberate exact-match lookup for a fixed audit

If the job is reproducing a fixed, already-closed batch of records exactly — not predicting new ones — fitting the sample exactly isn't overfitting; generalization was never the goal.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Judging capacity from training accuracy alone

Section 10's cubic model has the best training accuracy of the three and the worst true accuracy — training accuracy alone would rank it first.

12
CODE

The same three fits, computed instead of hand-worked

PYTHON · CONSTANT, LINEAR AND CUBIC FITS TO THE SAME FOUR POINTS 
import numpy as np

x_train = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4])
y_train = np.array([2, 4, 6, 9])       # x=4's true value is 8; this row is noisy
x_test = np.array([5, 6])
y_test = np.array([10, 12])            # never touched by any fit below

def fit_and_score(degree):
    # Vandermonde columns [x**degree, ..., x**1, x**0], solved by least squares —
    # the normal-equations mechanism, not a black-box "fit()" call
    A_train = np.vander(x_train, degree + 1)
    coeffs, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A_train, y_train, rcond=None)
    train_pred = A_train @ coeffs
    test_pred = np.vander(x_test, degree + 1) @ coeffs
    train_mse = np.mean((train_pred - y_train) ** 2)
    test_mse = np.mean((test_pred - y_test) ** 2)
    return train_mse, test_mse

for degree, label in [(0, "underfit"), (1, "reasonable fit"), (3, "overfit")]:
    train_mse, test_mse = fit_and_score(degree)
    print(f"degree {degree} ({label}): train_mse={train_mse:.4f}  test_mse={test_mse:.4f}")

# degree 0 (underfit):        train_mse=6.6875  test_mse=34.0625
# degree 1 (reasonable fit):  train_mse=0.0750  test_mse=1.3450
# degree 3 (overfit):         train_mse=0.0000  test_mse=58.0000

assert fit_and_score(3)[0] < 1e-9          # edge case: a degree-3 fit to 4 points is exact
assert fit_and_score(3)[1] > fit_and_score(0)[1]   # and still loses to the model that didn't try

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

SILENT UNDERFIT

Mediocre and mistaken for "as good as it gets"

Both errors are high but close together, and nobody checks whether a more expressive model would do better.

ZERO-ERROR TRAP

Perfect training score treated as success

A training metric near zero gets shipped without ever being checked against held-out data.

CAPACITY CREEP

Chasing the training score upward

Repeatedly increasing model complexity because training metrics improve, without watching what true error does.

SMALL-SEGMENT OVERFIT

Rare CFPB products, tiny per-product samples

A capacity setting that's reasonable overall can still overfit badly on the thinnest-data product segment.

UNBOUNDED FINE-TUNING

No early stopping on a language model

Continuing to fine-tune past the point where held-out performance peaks, purely because training loss keeps falling.

ONE-NUMBER DIAGNOSIS

Calling it from training error alone

Deciding a model is under- or overfitting without ever computing the other half of the pair.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Log training and held-out metrics side by side in MLflow, every run, never training metrics alone.
  • Treat a suspiciously perfect training score as a flag to investigate, not a milestone to announce.
  • Size regularization and depth limits to the smallest data segment a model has to serve, not the overall dataset.
  • Record the capacity choice — depth, degree, epochs, regularization strength — in the model registry alongside the metrics it produced.
  • Re-check for overfitting after retraining on new data, not just once at first launch.
  • Never grow model capacity solely because a training-set score improved; check the held-out number first.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

What error signature separates overfitting from underfitting?
Overfitting: training error low, true error high, large gap. Underfitting: both errors high and close together — no large gap, because neither number was ever going to be good.
Why can the same fixed model overfit on one dataset and underfit on another?
Capacity is relative to sample size, not absolute — a model flexible enough to overfit four points may not have nearly enough capacity to fit a genuinely complex pattern in four million.
Why doesn't more training data fix underfitting?
Underfitting comes from the hypothesis class being unable to express the true pattern; more data changes what the class is fit to, not what shapes the class can represent.
Why is CFPB Model 1's 636-value Company field specifically an overfitting risk?
An unconstrained tree has enough categories to carve out company-specific rules that fit training rows exactly, without those rules reflecting any real, repeatable driver of relief.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open one defensible reasoning pattern
(1) Run A: training 0.99, held-out 0.52 — a large gap between a near-perfect training score and a mediocre held-out one is exactly overfitting's signature. (2) No — Run B's training and held-out scores are both solid and close together, which is the profile of a reasonable fit, not a flaw to fix. (3) Reduce Run A's capacity: shallower trees, stronger regularization, or early stopping against the held-out set, then re-check both numbers together rather than trusting the training score alone.

The two error signatures

Underfitting: R̂ₙ(f) ≈ R(f), both ≫ R*. Overfitting: gap(f) = R(f) − R̂ₙ(f) large, with R̂ₙ(f) ≈ R* or lower.

Selection rule

  • Near-zero training error is a flag to check, not a win
  • Capacity is relative to sample size, never absolute

How would you explain overfitting to a non-technical stakeholder?
It's when a model gets suspiciously good at the exact examples it studied, by memorizing their quirks instead of learning the real pattern — so it stops working once it sees anything new.
A model has 99% training accuracy and 60% held-out accuracy. What do you check next, and why?
The generalization gap between the two numbers is the tell — next, check capacity (depth, regularization, degree) and whether the training sample is large enough to support that capacity, since this is overfitting's exact signature.
Give a concrete example of a small model overfitting a small dataset.
A degree-3 polynomial fit through exactly four training points, as in Section 10 — four parameters, four points, zero training error, and a true error worse than a constant prediction.
Why is "add more data" not a universal fix for a poorly performing model?
It only helps if the model is overfitting a too-small sample; if the model is underfitting because its hypothesis class can't express the pattern, more data of the same kind won't change what the class can represent.
