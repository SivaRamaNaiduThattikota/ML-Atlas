PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

The question before the checklist

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Four criteria for reaching for something other than ML

(1) A simple, deterministic rule already achieves the goal — no statistical model needed. (2) The problem requires 100%-guaranteed or fully-explainable behavior a statistical model cannot promise. (3) There is insufficient data to learn a real pattern, as distinct from noise. (4) The cost of an occasional wrong prediction is unacceptable relative to a rule-based fallback. Any one of these, on its own, can be decisive.

First memory: this is a decision about whether to reach for ML at all — not about doing ML well once you've decided to.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Every earlier concept assumed ML was already the right call

Concepts 01–09 all quietly assumed the decision to use ML had already been made correctly. That assumption can be wrong, and being wrong here costs more than any single checklist item, because it means the entire approach — not just one step of it — was the mistake.

ML DONE WELL, WRONG TOOL

Every checklist item from Concept 09 satisfied, on a problem that never needed ML in the first place.

THE RIGHT CALL, MADE FIRST

Deciding whether ML applies at all, before any checklist item is even reached.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Four requests, four different right answers

"Predict whether an email is spam" — genuinely learnable, ML is reasonable. "Compute a customer's tax withholding from their stated income bracket" — a fixed, deterministic table already exists; a model would just be an unnecessarily fragile approximation of arithmetic that's already exact. "Predict which of 50 labeled transactions out of ten million are fraudulent" — 50 positive examples is not enough signal to learn a real pattern from. "Decide dosing for a life-support device" — the domain demands guaranteed, fully-explainable behavior no statistical model can promise, regardless of accuracy.

ML FITS

Spam filtering — a genuinely learnable pattern, tolerant of occasional mistakes.

ML DOESN'T FIT

Tax withholding, life-support dosing, and the 50-example fraud case — each fails a different one of the four criteria.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Four independent tests, any one of which can decide it

1 · RULE ALREADY EXISTS

A deterministic rule already solves it

Tax withholding — an exact table, not a pattern to learn.

2 · GUARANTEE REQUIRED

The domain demands certainty ML can't offer

Life-support dosing — no accuracy level substitutes for a guarantee.

3 · INSUFFICIENT DATA

Too little signal to learn a real pattern

50 labeled fraud cases in ten million rows — not enough to learn from, only to overfit to.

4 · COST OF ERROR

A wrong prediction costs more than a rule-based fallback

When an occasional mistake is unacceptable and a simpler fallback exists, that fallback usually wins.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Where beginners collapse "hard" and "wrong tool" into one idea

TermWhat it actually isCommon confusion
"This problem is hard"Not one of the four criteria at allConfused with "ML is the wrong tool" — difficulty alone never disqualifies ML
Gate 1 (rule exists)A deterministic solution already existsConfused with "a simple model would also work" — the point is no model is needed at all
Gate 2 (guarantee required)The domain demands certainty, not just high accuracyConfused with "we need a very accurate model" — no accuracy level satisfies a guarantee requirement
Gate 3 (insufficient data)Too few examples to learn signal from noiseConfused with "not enough data yet, but more is coming" — a real, present-tense limitation, not a future plan
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often misunderstand

"If a problem is hard, ML must be the answer."

Difficulty alone doesn't imply learnability, and reaching for ML on a problem that fails Gate 1 or Gate 3 makes it harder, not easier.

"A high-accuracy model satisfies a guarantee requirement."

99% accuracy still means 1% wrong — Gate 2 exists precisely for domains where that 1% is unacceptable regardless of the number.

"50 examples is a small dataset, not a disqualifying one."

Whether 50 examples is enough depends entirely on the problem's real complexity — for ten million rows of transactions, it's Gate 3 failing, not just "small."

"These four gates are the same as Concept 09's checklist, just earlier."

Concept 09 assumes ML applies and checks whether it's framed well. These four gates decide whether it applies at all — a different question entirely.

04
INTUITION

Reaching for a hammer because it's the tool you know

A carpenter with a favorite hammer can still recognize when a job calls for a screwdriver instead — the hammer's quality isn't the question, its fitness for this particular job is. Learning ML well creates the same risk: a good tool, reached for on a job — a fixed rule, a guarantee-bound domain, too little data — it was never suited to.

Analogy boundaryChoosing the wrong hand tool wastes time. Choosing ML for a Gate 2 domain (a guarantee-required decision) can mean shipping a system that is confidently wrong in a way that matters — the stakes, not just the mismatch, are the real point.

09
MATHEMATICS

Gate 3, made precise

Gates 1, 2, and 4 are judgment calls this lesson deliberately keeps qualitative — whether a rule exists, whether a domain demands a guarantee, and whether an error's cost is acceptable are domain questions, not statistical ones. Gate 3 alone admits a rough quantitative check: for a rare-event problem with base rate p and n total examples, the expected count of positive examples is n·p. CFPB's real 22.3% relief rate against even a modest weekly pull gives plenty of positive examples; the fraud scenario's 50 positives out of 10,000,000 rows (p ≈ 0.000005) is the kind of count that makes distinguishing genuine pattern from sampling noise the real risk — not a bright line, but a magnitude check worth doing before assuming Gate 3 clears.

12
CODE

A four-gate checker, from scratch

PYTHON · CHECK ALL FOUR GATES FOR A DESCRIBED PROBLEM 
def should_use_ml(problem):
    if problem.get('deterministic_rule_exists', False):
        return False, 'Gate 1: a deterministic rule already solves this'
    if problem.get('requires_guarantee', False):
        return False, 'Gate 2: this domain requires certainty no model can promise'
    n_positive = problem.get('n_positive_examples', 0)
    if n_positive < problem.get('min_examples_needed', 30):
        return False, 'Gate 3: too few examples to learn a real pattern'
    if problem.get('error_cost_exceeds_fallback', False):
        return False, 'Gate 4: an occasional wrong prediction costs too much here'
    return True, 'Clears all four gates'

tax_withholding = {'deterministic_rule_exists': True}
fraud_labels = {'n_positive_examples': 50, 'min_examples_needed': 30}
spam_filter = {'n_positive_examples': 5000, 'min_examples_needed': 30}

print(should_use_ml(tax_withholding))   # (False, 'Gate 1: ...')
print(should_use_ml(fraud_labels))      # (True, 'Clears all four gates') -- 50 > 30, a deliberately naive threshold
print(should_use_ml(spam_filter))       # (True, 'Clears all four gates')

assert should_use_ml(tax_withholding)[0] == False
assert should_use_ml(spam_filter)[0] == True

Gate 3's fixed threshold above is a deliberate simplification for a runnable example — Section 09's real magnitude judgment (n·p against the problem's actual complexity) is the honest version, not a single constant.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

RULE-BLINDNESS

Building a model for a problem a rule already solves

Adds fragility and maintenance cost with no accuracy benefit over an exact rule.

GUARANTEE OVERREACH

Shipping a statistical model into a certainty-required domain

No accuracy number, however high, satisfies a genuine Gate 2 requirement.

NOISE-AS-SIGNAL

Training on Gate-3-failing data anyway

Produces a model that has learned sampling noise, not a real pattern — Concept 05's overfitting risk, guaranteed rather than merely possible.

COST-BLIND DEPLOYMENT

Ignoring Gate 4's fallback comparison

Deploying a model without ever pricing its error cost against the simpler alternative it's replacing.

REALITY

Production implications

  • Run these four gates during project intake or design review, before a data science team is assigned to a problem.
  • Re-check Gate 4 specifically whenever a model's error cost or its fallback's cost changes materially.
  • Document which gate a rejected-for-ML problem failed, so the decision doesn't get silently revisited without new information.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why doesn't a hard problem automatically justify using ML?
Difficulty isn't one of the four gates — a hard problem can still fail Gate 1 (a rule exists) or Gate 3 (insufficient data), regardless of how hard it looks.
Why can't a 99%-accurate model satisfy a Gate 2 requirement?
Gate 2 concerns guaranteed or fully-explainable behavior, which is independent of accuracy — any nonzero error rate can be disqualifying in a genuinely guarantee-required domain.
20
TRANSFER

Hands-on exercise

A team wants to build a model predicting which of a hospital's 40 million annual patient visits will result in a specific rare complication that has occurred exactly 12 times in the last five years of records. Decide which gate this fails, and state what you'd recommend instead.

21
FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open one defensible reasoning pattern
This fails Gate 3: 12 positive examples across five years of a 40-million-visit-per-year population is far too little signal to distinguish a genuine predictive pattern from noise. The honest recommendation is not "collect five more years and try again" as a fixed plan, but to work with clinical experts on a rule-based or checklist-driven flag for known risk factors, while continuing to accumulate cases, and revisit Gate 3 once enough genuine positive examples exist to make the judgment again.

Teach-back: explain in your own words why "the outcome is medically important" doesn't override Gate 3's data-sufficiency judgment.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you push back on a stakeholder who wants to use ML for a problem with an existing deterministic rule?
Name Gate 1 directly: a model would only approximate a rule that's already exact, adding fragility and maintenance cost with no accuracy upside.
Describe a domain where you'd refuse to deploy ML regardless of demonstrated accuracy.
Any Gate 2 domain — one requiring guaranteed or fully-explainable behavior, such as safety-critical dosing decisions, where no accuracy number substitutes for the guarantee itself.
How do you decide whether you have "enough" data for a rare-event problem?
Check the expected count of positive examples (n·p) against the problem's real complexity — a magnitude judgment, not a fixed threshold, as Section 09 shows.
