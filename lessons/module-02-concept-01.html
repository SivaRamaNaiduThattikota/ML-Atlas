PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

One honest number, two hidden questions

Concept 07 already produced one honest, correctly-baselined accuracy number that still meant almost nothing on its own: CFPB's majority-class floor, roughly 77.7% accuracy, catching zero of the real relief cases. This concept asks why that's possible at all — and shows it isn't a one-off. It's structural: accuracy alone can never say which kind of error is being made, or who it lands on.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Accuracy hides which kind of error, and to whom

Accuracy is one number: the fraction of predictions that were correct. It treats every mistake as identical — a real relief case that got denied counts exactly the same, inside that fraction, as a routine complaint that got wrongly flagged for review. Two models can report the identical accuracy while making completely different kinds of mistakes, on completely different people.

First memory: same accuracy number, different people bearing the cost.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 07 already proved the number can be honest and still empty

Concept 06 made the number honest — no peeking at the test set. Concept 07 made it judged — scored against a fixed baseline. Both disciplines were followed exactly when CFPB's majority-class floor was computed: roughly 77.7% accuracy, honestly measured, correctly named as a baseline. And it still says nothing about who was helped or hurt, because accuracy was never built to say that.

ONE HONEST NUMBER

A single fraction correct — no notion of which class an error fell in.

TWO HIDDEN QUESTIONS

Which kind of mistake happened? And who does that mistake actually affect?

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Two models, the identical 77.7%, opposite consequences

Score two models on the same 1,000-complaint test set, where 223 (22.3%) are real relief cases and 777 are not. Model A is Concept 07's majority-class floor: predicts "no relief" for everyone — 777 correct, 0 relief cases caught. Model B predicts relief for some complaints: it catches 100 of the 223 real relief cases, but wrongly flags 100 of the 777 non-relief complaints along the way. Model B's correct count is also 777 — its accuracy is also 77.7%, letter for letter the same as Model A's.

SAME NUMBER

Model A and Model B both report exactly 77.7% accuracy on the identical test set.

DIFFERENT REALITY

Model A helps nobody and bothers nobody. Model B helps 100 real relief cases and burdens 100 people who didn't need review — a completely different system.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Accuracy adds correct predictions without asking which class they came from

1 · ONE DENOMINATOR

Every row counts the same

1,000 complaints, one denominator — accuracy never separates the 223 relief rows from the 777 non-relief rows.

2 · ONE NUMERATOR

Every correct prediction counts the same

A correctly-denied non-relief complaint and a correctly-approved relief case are added into the identical numerator.

3 · TWO WAYS TO BE WRONG, COLLAPSED

Missed cases and false alarms look identical to accuracy

Denying a deserving complaint and flagging an undeserving one both just subtract one from the numerator — accuracy can't tell which happened.

4 · SAME SCORE, MANY ERROR MIXES

Many different error compositions produce one accuracy

Any split of a fixed number of wrong predictions between the two kinds of mistake yields the same accuracy, as CFPB's 77.7% shows twice over.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often misunderstand

"If accuracy is honest and beats the baseline, the model is fine."

CFPB's own numbers show two 77.7%-accuracy, correctly-baselined models with opposite real-world behavior — clearing both disciplines from Concepts 06 and 07 still leaves this question open.

"A higher accuracy is always the better model."

Not if the higher number comes from catching more of the wrong kind of case, or from a class-imbalanced test set that rewards ignoring the class that matters.

"This is the same as the baseline discipline, just phrased differently."

Concept 07 asked whether a number clears a floor. This concept asks what the number itself is structurally unable to say, even once it clears every floor.

"Accuracy is a bad metric and should be avoided."

Accuracy isn't wrong, it's incomplete — one honest measurement that needs a second kind of measurement beside it, which Concept 02 begins supplying.

04
INTUITION

A single exam score with no answer key attached

A test-taker's overall percentage tells you they got, say, 78 of 100 questions right — but not which 22 they missed, or whether those misses clustered on one topic a hiring decision actually depends on. Accuracy is that same single percentage: correct either way you compute it, and silent about the shape of the 22.3 points it lost.

Analogy boundaryAn exam's "which 22 questions" is discoverable by simply rereading the exam. A production model's "which kind of row" requires deliberately splitting the count by class — that's Concept 02's job, not a free byproduct of the accuracy number itself.

06
MECHANISM

Two routes to the identical numerator

Take CFPB's 1,000-row test set: 223 real relief cases, 777 non-relief complaints
Model A (majority-class): predicts "no relief" for all 1,000 → 777 correct on non-relief, 0 correct on relief → accuracy 777/1000 = 77.7%
Model B: misses 123 of the 223 relief cases and wrongly flags 100 of the 777 non-relief complaints → correct = (223 − 123) relief + (777 − 100) non-relief = 100 + 677 = 777 → accuracy also 777/1000 = 77.7%
Both totals land on the identical numerator, 777, by two completely different routes through the 1,000 rows
The accuracy formula has no step that could tell these two routes apart — it only ever sees the final count

09
MATHEMATICS

Accuracy, made precise — and where its precision stops

Let n be the size of the test set and c the number of predictions where the model's output matched the true label. Accuracy is defined plainly as c divided by n. Section 06's arithmetic shows the identical c = 777 arising from two different partitions of the 1,000 rows — 777 correct-on-non-relief-only for Model A, versus 100 correct-on-relief plus 677 correct-on-non-relief for Model B. The finer split — how many of each true class were predicted correctly, and how many incorrectly, in which direction — has no name or symbol yet in this course; that decomposition, and the vocabulary for it, is exactly what Concept 02's confusion matrix formalizes. This lesson deliberately stops at showing the split exists and that accuracy alone cannot see it.

Acc = c / n — where c depends only on the count of matches, never on which class each match belonged to

12
CODE

Two models, one accuracy, proven in NumPy

PYTHON · SAME ACCURACY, OPPOSITE ERROR COMPOSITION 
import numpy as np

n_relief, n_no_relief = 223, 777
y_true = np.array([1] * n_relief + [0] * n_no_relief)

def score(y_true, y_pred):
    accuracy = np.mean(y_true == y_pred)
    relief_rows = y_true == 1
    caught = np.sum(y_pred[relief_rows] == 1)
    false_alarms = np.sum(y_pred[~relief_rows] == 1)
    return accuracy, caught, false_alarms

majority = np.zeros(1000, dtype=int)     # Model A: predicts "no relief" for everyone

model_b = y_true.copy()
model_b[:123] = 0                        # miss 123 of the 223 real relief cases
model_b[223:323] = 1                     # wrongly flag 100 of the 777 non-relief complaints

acc_a, caught_a, fa_a = score(y_true, majority)
acc_b, caught_b, fa_b = score(y_true, model_b)

print(f"Model A: accuracy {acc_a:.1%}, caught {caught_a}/223, false alarms {fa_a}")
print(f"Model B: accuracy {acc_b:.1%}, caught {caught_b}/223, false alarms {fa_b}")
# Model A: accuracy 77.7%, caught 0/223, false alarms 0
# Model B: accuracy 77.7%, caught 100/223, false alarms 100

assert acc_a == acc_b == 0.777
assert caught_a == 0 and caught_b == 100
assert fa_a == 0 and fa_b == 100

Model B's split — 123 missed, 100 false alarms — is one of many ways to land on 777 correct. Section 14's lab lets you slide continuously between two smaller-scale extremes of the identical idea.

13
ECOSYSTEM

Library authors already correct for this gap

sklearn.metrics.accuracy_score computes exactly the c/n fraction Section 09 defines, and nothing more — it takes only y_true and y_pred, with no parameter that could report which class an error belonged to. sklearn.metrics.balanced_accuracy_score exists specifically because plain accuracy is misleading on imbalanced classes: it averages recall computed separately per class, which requires exactly the per-class breakdown accuracy itself doesn't have. That per-class breakdown is Concept 02's subject, not built here — balanced_accuracy_score is flagged forward as evidence the gap is real and library authors already routinely correct for it, not as this lesson's tool.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Never report accuracy alone in a model card or launch review — pair it with at least the per-class breakdown Concept 02 introduces.
  • Class-imbalanced production data, like CFPB's 22.3% relief rate, makes this illusion routine, not rare — expect it by default on any rare-outcome problem.
  • A monitoring dashboard that only tracks aggregate accuracy can look stable while one class's error rate silently drifts, flagged forward to Module 17.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why can two models score identical accuracy on the identical test set while behaving completely differently in production?
Accuracy sums correct predictions without conditioning on which class they belonged to, so any split of a fixed number of correct and incorrect predictions across classes produces the same fraction.
Why isn't clearing Concept 07's baseline enough to call a model ready?
Clearing a floor says the number beat a fixed comparison point; it says nothing about which kind of error is still occurring or who it affects — a separate, unaddressed question.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open one defensible reasoning pattern
The 0.4-point lift only confirms the candidate beats Concept 07's floor — it says nothing about whether that lift came from catching more real relief cases, catching fewer, or trading one for the other via more false alarms. The right question is "how many of the real relief cases does it actually catch, and at what false-alarm cost?" — exactly the per-class count Concept 02's confusion matrix will name and provide.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you explain to a non-technical stakeholder why 95% accuracy might still mean the model is nearly useless?
If the relevant outcome occurs in under 5% of cases, predicting "never" alone can already reach 95%, exactly like CFPB's 77.7% floor from a 22.3% relief rate — the number rewards ignoring the rare class.
Describe two models with identical accuracy that you would refuse to treat as equivalent.
CFPB's own pair — a majority-class floor catching 0 of 223 relief cases, and a partial-catch model catching 100 of 223 at the cost of 100 false alarms — same 77.7%, opposite systems.
What's the minimum additional information needed to distinguish two same-accuracy classifiers?
A per-class breakdown of correct and incorrect predictions — exactly what Concept 02's confusion matrix formalizes.
Why does class imbalance make this problem worse rather than causing it?
The blind spot exists on any classifier, but a skewed class distribution lets the majority-class strategy alone reach a high number, making the illusion more convincing and more common in practice.
