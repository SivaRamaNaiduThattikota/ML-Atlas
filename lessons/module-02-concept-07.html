01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Choosing a metric means answering five questions, not inventing a sixth

Concepts 01-06 handed you six ways to score a model: accuracy, the confusion matrix underneath it, precision/recall/F1, ROC-AUC/PR-AUC, regression's MAE/RMSE/R², and calibration's Brier score. None of them said which one your model should actually be judged by — that depends entirely on the business problem behind it. "Choosing a metric" is the name for that separate decision: given what a model gets wrong and who it costs, which of the six is the one to tune against, which one is a guardrail, and which one is just worth watching.

First memory: the six metrics don't compete with each other — the business problem picks which one leads, and the rest usually still have a job.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Without this, the metric gets picked by accident

Left undecided, a team defaults to whichever number a library prints first — usually accuracy — or whichever number looks most impressive in a slide, usually a high ROC-AUC. Concept 01 already showed accuracy can hide who a model helps and hurts; Concept 04 already showed ROC-AUC can look strong while precision stays weak at every usable cutoff. Neither failure is about the metric being wrong — it's about the metric being picked for the wrong reason.

PICKING A METRIC BY DEFAULT

Whatever the library prints, or whatever looks best in a slide — accuracy and ROC-AUC both win this way, regardless of whether they fit the problem.

PICKING A METRIC BY THIS CONCEPT'S PROCESS

Whichever number the actual costs, class balance and deployment stage point to — sometimes accuracy or ROC-AUC really is the right answer, but only after being checked, not by default.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Model B's numbers, revisited as a decision instead of a fact

Concepts 02-03 already produced Model B's matrix on CFPB's 1,000-row test set: TP=100, FP=100, FN=123, TN=677 — precision 50%, recall 44.8%, F1 ≈47.3%. Those three numbers were reported as facts back then. Here they become the input to a decision: Concept 03 already established that a false negative denies a deserving complainant while a false positive burns a reviewer's time. If those two costs really are different, is 47.3% F1 — built on the assumption they're equal — actually the number Model 1 should be tuned to raise?

WHAT F1 ASSUMES

Precision and recall matter equally

F1's harmonic mean (Concept 03) treats a one-point gain in either ratio as worth the same — a built-in 50/50 weighting.

WHAT CFPB'S COSTS SAY

They don't matter equally

A missed relief case (low recall) costs a real complainant; a false alarm (low precision) costs reviewer time — two different audiences, two different weights.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Tuning metric, guardrail, and companion — three different jobs

TermWhat it actually isCommon confusion
Tuning metricThe single number a threshold or a model-selection decision is directly optimized againstAssumed to be the only metric that matters, once chosen
Guardrail metricA floor or ceiling that must hold no matter what the tuning metric says — e.g. a minimum precision so reviewer load stays boundedConfused with the tuning metric itself — a guardrail is never the thing being maximized, only the thing that must not break
Companion metricTracked for information, not directly optimized — calibration (Concept 06) while Model 1's probability isn't read literally yetDismissed as irrelevant just because it isn't the tuning target — it can become one the moment its condition changes
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often misunderstand

"F1 is the safe, neutral default."

It isn't neutral — it hard-codes an equal-cost assumption Concept 03's complainant/reviewer split doesn't actually have.

"Whichever metric scores highest is the right one to report."

The metric has to match the decision first — a high number on the wrong metric is still the wrong metric.

"Pick one metric and never look at the others."

A tuning metric almost always needs a guardrail alongside it, and sometimes a companion — three jobs, not one.

"This decision is permanent."

It depends on the cost ratio, the class balance and the deployment stage — Concept 08 shows what happens when a metric outlives the conditions that justified it.

04
INTUITION

A diagnostic scanner with six readouts

A mechanic's diagnostic scanner can report six different readouts at once — torque, emissions, brake-pad wear, tire pressure, battery voltage, fuel efficiency. None of those six is "the" number a mechanic watches; a race team cares about torque and tire pressure, a family buying a used car cares about brake wear and emissions, and a delivery fleet cares about fuel efficiency above everything else. The scanner didn't change between those three buyers — only which readout gets acted on, and which ones stay as guardrails, changed. Concepts 01-06 are the scanner; this concept is choosing the buyer.

Analogy boundaryA car's readouts are mostly independent of each other in a way metrics aren't always — improving Model 1's recall can actively cost it precision (Concept 04's whole threshold-sweep point), where a mechanic's torque reading doesn't trade off against tire pressure the same way. The analogy explains "different buyers, different readouts," not the specific trade-off arithmetic, which Concepts 03-04 already covered.

09
MATHEMATICS

The decision rule, formalized — not a new metric

Concepts 01-06 already proved every formula this concept touches. The only new formal object here is the selection rule that picks among them. When candidates are still being compared, the rule is simply pick the candidate with the highest PR-AUC (or ROC-AUC, near balance). Once a threshold is being fixed for a chosen model, the rule becomes a constrained optimization: t* = argmax over every threshold t where precision(t) ≥ p_min, of recall(t) — read as "the highest-recall threshold among every threshold that still clears the stated precision floor." Both precision(t) and recall(t) are exactly Concept 03's ratios, evaluated at a moving cutoff exactly the way Concept 04 already swept them; nothing about either function is new.

t* = argmax { recall(t) : precision(t) ≥ p_min, t ∈ thresholds } — the floor p_min comes from a stakeholder, not from this formula

CODE

The constrained threshold selector, from scratch

PYTHON · RECALL-MAXIMIZING THRESHOLD UNDER A PRECISION FLOOR 
import numpy as np

# A small illustrative scored set -- not Model 1's real predictions, just enough
# rows to show the SELECTION RULE. Precision and recall themselves are Concept
# 03's formulas, reused exactly, not re-derived here.
scores = np.array([0.92, 0.85, 0.78, 0.66, 0.61, 0.55, 0.44, 0.38, 0.21, 0.12])
actual = np.array([1,    1,    0,    1,    0,    1,    0,    0,    1,    0])

def precision_recall_at(threshold, scores, actual):
    predicted = scores >= threshold
    tp = np.sum(predicted & (actual == 1))
    fp = np.sum(predicted & (actual == 0))
    fn = np.sum((~predicted) & (actual == 1))
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) else 0.0
    recall = tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) else 0.0
    return precision, recall

def select_threshold(scores, actual, precision_floor):
    """Section 09's t* = argmax{recall(t) : precision(t) >= p_min} rule.
    Among every threshold that clears the floor, keep the one with the
    highest recall. No metric formula is new -- only this selection step."""
    candidates = []
    for t in sorted(set(scores)):
        precision, recall = precision_recall_at(t, scores, actual)
        if precision >= precision_floor:
            candidates.append((t, precision, recall))
    if not candidates:
        return None  # no threshold clears the floor -- the floor itself needs revisiting
    return max(candidates, key=lambda row: row[2])

best = select_threshold(scores, actual, precision_floor=0.60)
print(f"Chosen threshold {best[0]:.2f}: precision {best[1]:.2f}, recall {best[2]:.2f}")
# Chosen threshold 0.55: precision 0.67, recall 0.80

assert best is not None
assert round(best[0], 2) == 0.55
assert select_threshold(scores, actual, precision_floor=1.5) is None  # impossible floor

ECOSYSTEM

The library version

sklearn.metrics.precision_recall_curve computes exactly the (threshold, precision, recall) triples Section 12's loop builds by hand, across every threshold at once. Wrapping the same constrained rule into a real hyperparameter search — via make_scorer on a custom function passed to GridSearchCV's scoring parameter, or simply scoring='average_precision' for the pre-threshold PR-AUC comparison stage — operationalizes Section 09's rule so a real search over candidates, not just a hand loop over ten rows, respects the same guardrail.

    DIAGNOSIS

    Failure modes

    METRIC LAUNDERING

    Swapping the tuning metric mid-project without re-justifying it

    A model quietly re-tuned from PR-AUC to accuracy because the accuracy number looks better in a status update — the five questions haven't changed, so neither should the answer.

    GUARDRAIL-FREE TUNING

    Maximizing recall with no stated precision floor

    Reviewer queues overflow the moment recall is pushed with nothing bounding the false-alarm rate it trades against.

    COMPANION NEGLECT

    Never rechecking a companion metric once its condition changes

    Calibration stops being optional the day someone starts averaging Model 1's raw probability for a staffing estimate — if nobody notices that day, the companion metric never gets promoted.

    ONE METRIC FOR EVERY MODEL

    Reusing Model 1's tuning metric for Model 2 without re-running the filter

    Model 2's ranking-only job (Concept 06) answers Q3-Q5 differently than Model 1's — the filter has to be run per model, not once for the whole project.

    Misconceptions

    Incorrect beliefCorrection

    REALITY

    Production implications

    • Record the tuning metric, the guardrail and the companion metric together in the model registry (Module 18) — as a stated decision with its five answers attached, not just a single logged number.
    • Re-run the five-question filter whenever a condition changes — a shift in class balance, a new stated cost ratio, or a move from "comparing candidates" to "deployed" can all change the answer, exactly the drift Module 17's monitoring is built to catch.
    • Treat a guardrail breach post-deployment as a hard stop, not a soft warning — the guardrail exists specifically because the tuning metric alone would keep optimizing straight through it.
    19
    RECALL

    Knowledge check

    Why can't F1 be treated as a universally safe default metric?
    F1 hard-codes an equal weighting between precision and recall — safe only when a business problem's two error types really do cost the same, which Q3 of the filter checks rather than assumes.
    You're comparing three untuned candidate models on an imbalanced target with no deployment threshold fixed yet. Which metric leads that comparison, and why not accuracy or a fixed-threshold precision/recall?
    PR-AUC — accuracy hides the imbalance and the FP/FN split (Concept 01), and precision/recall need an operating threshold that doesn't exist yet (Concept 03); PR-AUC summarizes ranking quality across every threshold at once (Concept 04).
    A model's probability is only ever compared to a fixed cutoff, never averaged or read literally anywhere downstream. Does calibration need to be its tuning metric?
    No — calibration matters when the literal probability value is consumed (Concept 06); here only the threshold behavior matters, so calibration stays at most a companion to watch, not the metric being optimized.

    23
    CAREER PRACTICE

    Interview questions

    A stakeholder says "just optimize for whichever metric has the highest number." Why is that not a valid strategy?
    A high number only means something once the metric matches the decision — an impressive ROC-AUC or accuracy score can coexist with a model that's badly wrong for the actual costs involved, exactly what Concepts 01 and 04 already demonstrated.
    Coding: given a confusion matrix at several candidate thresholds, how would you select the one that maximizes recall without letting precision fall below a stated floor?
    Compute precision and recall at each threshold (Concept 03's formulas), filter out any threshold whose precision falls below the floor, then take the highest-recall survivor — exactly Section 12's select_threshold function.
    Debugging: a team tuned Model 1 purely for recall and reviewer complaints spiked immediately after deployment. What was missing?
    A guardrail — a stated minimum-precision floor that the recall-maximizing search should never have been allowed to cross, per Section b06's distinction between a tuning metric and a guardrail.
    System design: design the post-deployment monitoring for Model 1's chosen metrics. What gets tracked, and what triggers a review?
    Track the tuning metric (recall at the deployed threshold), the guardrail (precision, with an alert if it dips below its floor), and the companion (calibration, escalated to a guardrail the moment the raw probability starts getting consumed literally) — tying into Module 17's drift monitoring for when any of them starts moving.
