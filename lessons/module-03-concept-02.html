01 · CONCEPT FIRST

One variable's shape, then two variables' relationship

Univariate analysis examines a single variable alone -- not just its mean, but the full shape of its values: where they cluster, how spread out they are, whether there's one lump or several. Bivariate analysis examines two variables together -- whether they tend to rise and fall in step, whether one category of a field associates with a different outcome than another. "Uni-" and "bi-" name exactly what they sound like: one column at a time, then two columns at a time. Neither one yet asks about three or more columns together -- that's multivariate analysis, a later idea this concept only sets up.

First memory: univariate asks "what does this one column look like?" Bivariate asks "do these two columns move together?" Both questions come before any question about three or more.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Four tools, two questions

1 · HISTOGRAM

Bin one numeric variable, count each bin

Split the variable's range into equal-width intervals and count how many values fall in each -- the tool that turned "573.44" into "two clusters and a gap."

2 · CENTER & SPREAD

Mean, median, min, max -- read together, not alone

The mean and median can disagree (a hint of skew); the min and max show the full range a single average can't reveal on its own.

3 · SCATTER PLOT

Plot two numeric variables as points

Each complaint becomes one dot: narrative length on one axis, resolution time on the other. A rising cloud of dots suggests the two move together; a flat, shapeless cloud suggests they don't.

4 · CROSS-TAB & CORRELATION

A count table for two categories, a number for two numerics

A cross-tab counts every combination of two categorical variables (product type × outcome). Correlation compresses a scatter plot's linear trend into one number between -1 and 1.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Four things beginners fold into "just plot it"

TermWhat it actually isCommon confusion
Univariate vs. bivariate vs. multivariateOne column, two columns, three-or-more columns -- strictly by countMultivariate analysis is only named here as a later idea; nothing in this concept builds it
Histogram vs. bar chartA histogram bins a continuous numeric range; a bar chart counts discrete categories that already existBoth look like bars -- a histogram's bin edges are a choice you make, a bar chart's categories are not
Correlation vs. causationCorrelation measures whether two variables move together; it says nothing about whyA high correlation number tempts a causal story -- Concept 06, "Correlation versus causation traps," is where that trap gets named properly; this concept only flags it
Scatter plot vs. cross-tabScatter plots pair two numeric variables; cross-tabs pair two categorical variablesBoth are "bivariate," but a numeric pair and a categorical pair need two different tools, never the same one
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"I already have the mean, so I don't need a histogram."

Section 03's sixteen narratives share one mean but two clusters -- the mean is a valid number and still an incomplete picture.

"A high correlation number means one thing is causing the other."

Correlation only measures whether two variables move together. Concept 06 covers why that's not the same as causation -- this concept just plots the relationship and reports the number.

"More histogram bins always shows more truth."

Too few bins can hide a real gap; too many can turn a real pattern into sixteen individual data points that look like noise. Section 14's lab makes this trade-off directly visible.

"A scatter plot and a cross-tab are interchangeable."

A cross-tab needs categories to count; a scatter plot needs two numeric axes to place a dot on. Forcing narrative length into a cross-tab, or product type into a scatter plot, doesn't work.

A univariate summary is a portrait: everything you can say about one subject in isolation -- their height, their build, how they compare to an "average" person. 05
PRECISION

Formal definitions

TermDefinitionBoundary
Univariate analysisExamining the distribution, central tendency, and spread of a single variable in isolationSays nothing about how that variable relates to any other
HistogramA plot that divides a numeric variable's range into equal-width bins and counts observations per binBin count and bin width are choices, not properties of the data -- Section 14's lab makes the consequence of that choice concrete
Bivariate analysisExamining the relationship between exactly two variablesDistinct from multivariate analysis (three or more variables at once), which this concept only names
Pearson correlation coefficient (r)A number between -1 and 1 summarizing the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two numeric variablesMeasures association, never causation; also blind to any relationship that isn't roughly linear
Cross-tabulation (contingency table)A table of counts for every combination of two categorical variables' valuesThe categorical-pair equivalent of a scatter plot -- not interchangeable with it
06
MECHANISM

Building each tool, step by step

Histogram — find min and max, choose a bin count k, divide the range into k equal-width intervals, count how many values fall in each.
Center & spread — compute mean and median (do they agree?), then min, max, and the range between them.
Scatter plot — for two numeric variables, plot each row as one point: variable A on the x-axis, variable B on the y-axis.
Cross-tab — for two categorical variables, build a grid of counts, one cell per combination of categories.
Correlation — center both numeric variables on their own means, multiply the deviations pairwise, sum, and normalize by each variable's own spread.

09
MATHEMATICS

Center, spread, and one new formula for two variables

Mean and median are already familiar. The only new formula this concept introduces is the Pearson correlation coefficient, which measures how tightly two numeric variables move together in a straight-line sense.

r = Σ(xᵢ - x̄)(yᵢ - ȳ) / √( Σ(xᵢ - x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ - ȳ)² )
SymbolMeaning
xᵢ, yᵢThe two paired values for row i -- e.g. one complaint's narrative length and its resolution time
x̄, ȳThe mean of all x values, and the mean of all y values
NumeratorHow much x and y deviate from their own means together -- large and same-signed when both variables tend to rise together
DenominatorEach variable's own spread, multiplied together -- this is what keeps r bounded between -1 and 1 regardless of the variables' original units

r = 1 means a perfect increasing straight-line relationship; r = -1 means a perfect decreasing one; r = 0 means no linear relationship -- which is not the same as no relationship at all.

x̄ = 490, ȳ = 6.75. Summing the last column: Σ(xᵢ-x̄)(yᵢ-ȳ) = 5,550. Summing the squared deviations separately: Σ(xᵢ-x̄)² = 598,600 and Σ(yᵢ-ȳ)² = 59.5. So:

r = 5,550 / √(598,600 × 59.5) = 5,550 / √35,616,700 ≈ 5,550 / 5,968.0 ≈ 0.930

r ≈ 0.93 -- a strong positive linear relationship. Longer narratives in this small sample took noticeably longer to resolve. That is exactly what the number claims, and nothing more: it does not say a long narrative causes a slow resolution, only that the two moved together across these eight rows.

CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a production use, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

CFPB's product-type counts and narrative lengths

A bar chart of complaint counts per product, and a histogram of narrative lengths -- both univariate, both immediately useful.

BOUNDARY CASE

A variable with zero spread

If every sampled complaint came from one product category, a scatter plot or correlation against it is undefined -- there's no variation to relate to anything.

PRODUCTION USE

A standing EDA dashboard, rebuilt on every new data pull

The same histogram-plus-scatter-plus-cross-tab routine, re-run automatically whenever new complaint data lands, not just once at project kickoff.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Trusting r = 0.93 without ever looking at the scatter plot

Anscombe's quartet -- referenced back in Concept 01 -- proves datasets can share an identical correlation while looking nothing alike; the plot is not optional.

12
CODE

Histogram bins and correlation, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
lengths = [105, 130, 150, 170, 190, 210, 230, 250, 780, 830, 880, 930, 980, 1040, 1110, 1190]

def histogram(values, k):
    lo, hi = min(values), max(values)
    width = (hi - lo) / k
    counts = [0] * k
    for v in values:
        idx = min(int((v - lo) / width), k - 1)
        counts[idx] += 1
    return counts

def pearson_r(xs, ys):
    n = len(xs)
    mx, my = sum(xs) / n, sum(ys) / n
    sxy = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys))
    sxx = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
    syy = sum((y - my) ** 2 for y in ys)
    return sxy / (sxx * syy) ** 0.5

assert histogram(lengths, 4) == [8, 0, 3, 5]          # the gap Section 03's figure shows

x = [120, 200, 300, 400, 500, 650, 800, 950]
y = [3, 5, 4, 7, 6, 9, 8, 12]
r = pearson_r(x, y)
assert round(r, 2) == 0.93                             # matches Section 10's hand calculation

# Edge case: a variable with no spread breaks correlation outright
try:
    pearson_r([5, 5, 5], [1, 2, 3])
except ZeroDivisionError:
    print("undefined -- no variation in x to correlate against")

Both functions reuse only sums, differences, and one square root -- the identical arithmetic Section 09 wrote out symbolically and Section 10 ran by hand.

DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

BIN-WIDTH BLINDNESS

Trusting whatever bin count a plotting library defaults to

Section 14's lab shows the identical sixteen values reading as one lump, two clusters, or noise, purely from bin count.

CORRELATION OVERREACH

Treating r ≈ 0.93 as proof of a causal mechanism

The number describes eight rows moving together; it says nothing about why -- Concept 06 is where that question actually gets addressed.

WRONG TOOL FOR THE PAIR

Forcing a categorical field into a scatter plot, or a numeric one into a cross-tab

Section 15's table exists specifically to prevent this mismatch.

SUMMARY-ONLY BIVARIATE

Reporting r without ever drawing the scatter plot

Anscombe's quartet is the standing counterexample: identical correlation, visibly different relationships.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • A dashboard that reports correlation numbers without the underlying scatter plots invites exactly the overreach Section 16 names -- both belong together, every time.
  • Bin-width choices for a histogram should follow a stated rule (a fixed count, or a rule like Sturges') rather than whatever a plotting library defaults to, so two people looking at the same data see the same shape.
  • Cross-tabs computed on a live product taxonomy need to be re-run whenever categories are added or renamed -- a stale cross-tab can look stable while the underlying categories have already shifted.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why did the sixteen narrative lengths' mean (573.44) sit inside an empty histogram bin?
Because the sixteen values form two separate clusters -- eight short, eight long -- with nothing actually near their shared midpoint; a mean is a single number, blind to that gap.
What does a Pearson correlation of 0.93 claim, and what does it not claim?
It claims narrative length and days-to-resolve moved together strongly and roughly linearly across the eight sampled complaints. It does not claim one caused the other, and it says nothing about any relationship that isn't roughly straight-line shaped.

The rule

  • Bivariate: two variables' relationship -- a scatter plot and correlation for numeric pairs, a cross-tab for categorical pairs

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you explain the difference between univariate and bivariate analysis to a non-technical stakeholder?
Univariate looks at one measurement on its own -- like everyone's height in a room. Bivariate looks at whether two measurements move together -- like height and shoe size across the same room.
A colleague reports "r = 0.93 between narrative length and resolution time, so long complaints cause slow resolutions." How do you respond?
Correlation describes how two variables move together, not why. A confound (complex cases produce both long narratives and slow resolutions) or reverse direction is just as consistent with r = 0.93 -- Concept 06 covers how to actually investigate the "why."
Why might two datasets share an identical correlation coefficient while looking completely different when plotted?
Pearson's r only captures the linear component of a relationship -- Anscombe's quartet demonstrates four visually distinct datasets sharing the same correlation, mean, and variance.
System design: how would you make histogram bin-width choices consistent across a team's dashboards?
Fix a stated rule (a constant bin count, or a formula like Sturges' or Freedman-Diaconis) in a shared plotting utility, rather than letting each analyst accept whatever a library defaults to -- Section 14's lab shows how much that default silently changes the reading.
