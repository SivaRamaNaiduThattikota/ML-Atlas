MODULE 03 · CONCEPT 03

The value isn't there.
Ask why.

Concept 02 built a histogram, a scatter plot, and a cross-tab -- and quietly assumed every cell it touched actually had a value in it. This concept drops that assumption. When a field is missing, it is missing for a reason, and that reason falls into exactly three formal categories: MCAR, missing for a reason unrelated to anything else in the data; MAR, missing for a reason an observed column already explains; and MNAR, missing for a reason tied to the very value that didn't get recorded. This concept only diagnoses which one you're looking at -- what to do about it is a later module's decision, not this one's.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

MCAR, MAR, and MNAR -- three answers to "missing for what reason?"

Every missing value in a dataset has some reason behind it, even if that reason is never written down. MCAR (missing completely at random) means that reason has nothing to do with anything in the data -- not the missing value itself, not any other column. MAR (missing at random) means the reason is fully explained by a column you can already see -- once you know that column, the missingness stops looking mysterious. MNAR (missing not at random) means the reason is tied to the value that's actually missing -- the one thing you can't look up to check. These three names classify a pattern of absence, not a data-cleaning technique.

First memory: MCAR depends on nothing. MAR depends on something you can see. MNAR depends on the very thing you can't see. Same order, every time you meet this trio again.

Eight complaints, a resolution-notes field, and a gap that tracks a column you can see

CFPB case handlers write a short resolution-notes entry once a complaint closes. Pull eight closed complaints and check whether that field actually got filled in: four came in by phone, four by web. Phone: notes missing on three of four. Web: notes missing on just one of four.

PHONE · 4 COMPLAINTS

WEB · 4 COMPLAINTS

1 missing note

Only 25% of web-channel complaints are missing the same field.

Nothing about this pattern requires knowing what any specific missing note would have said. Channel alone -- a column that's fully observed -- already explains most of the gap. Hold onto that distinction; Section 04 gives it a name, and gives the other two mechanisms their own concrete shape too.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

One question, asked twice

STEP 1

Does the missing rate change across an observed column?

Split the field's missing/present flag by a column you already have -- channel, product, complaint outcome -- and compare rates. Section 03's 75% vs. 25% is exactly this check.

STEP 2

If it's flat everywhere you check, suspect MCAR

No observed column moves the missing rate. The gap looks the same no matter which slice of the data you're standing in.

STEP 3

If it moves with an observed column, that's MAR

Section 03's channel split is the textbook shape: once you condition on channel, the mystery is gone.

STEP 4

If it still seems to depend on the value itself, suspect MNAR

No observed column fully explains the gap, and the likeliest reason left standing is the unrecorded value itself -- the one thing you cannot check directly.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Four things beginners fold into "it's just missing"

TermWhat it actually isCommon confusion
MCAR vs. MAR vs. MNARWhat the missingness depends on -- nothing, an observed column, or the missing value itselfTreated as three levels of "how much" is missing, when they're actually three different reasons why, independent of the missing rate's size
Missing value vs. outlierA missing value has no recorded number at all; an outlier has a recorded number that's extremeBoth get called "bad data" -- Concept 04 is where an actually-present extreme value gets handled, not here
The value vs. its missingness indicatorThe value itself may be unobserved, but "was this row missing it?" (yes/no) is a fully observed column of its ownAssuming a missingness flag is itself "more missing data," when it's actually complete, usable information
Diagnosis vs. imputationThis concept only names which mechanism produced the gap; deciding how to fill, drop, or flag it is Module 04's jobJumping straight to "so I'll just fill it with the mean" before ever asking why it's missing in the first place
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Only 5% of this field is missing, so the mechanism doesn't matter."

A small missing rate concentrated in exactly the cases you care about most -- Section 11's mistake card gives a concrete CFPB version -- can matter far more than a large, evenly-spread one.

"I checked one column and the missing rate looked flat, so it must be MCAR."

Flat against one observed column only rules out that column. A different observed column might still explain the gap -- Section 16 names this trap directly.

"I can prove a field is MNAR by studying the data I have."

MNAR is defined by a dependence on the value you don't have. No amount of staring at the columns you do have can fully rule it out -- that's exactly what makes it the hardest of the three.

"Dropping every row with a missing value is always the safe default."

Safe under MCAR; under MAR or MNAR it can quietly delete a non-random slice of the data and bias whatever gets trained on what's left -- Module 04 is where the actual fix gets chosen, but the mechanism decides whether dropping is even defensible.

04
INTUITION

An empty seat at a dinner table

Someone's seat at a dinner table is empty. If it's empty because a coin flip decided who'd be running late that day, the empty seat means nothing about anyone at the table -- that's MCAR. If it's empty because the guest works the night shift and always misses this seating, you already know that about them from something you can see on their calendar -- that's MAR. If it's empty because the guest is deliberately avoiding someone else at the table, and you'd only learn that by asking the one question nobody's answering, that's MNAR -- the reason is locked inside the very fact you don't have.

Analogy boundaryThe empty seat captures "three different reasons for an absence." It doesn't capture how you'd actually check any of the three against real columns -- Sections 06 and 09-10 are the real mechanism; the seat is only the shape of the idea.
05
PRECISION

Formal definitions

TermDefinitionBoundary
Missingness indicator (R)A fully observed binary flag: 1 if a given field's value is missing for a row, 0 if it's presentR is never itself missing -- it's the object every mechanism actually describes the behavior of
MCARR is statistically independent of both the observed data and the missing values themselvesThe strongest, least common assumption -- Section 09 states it formally
MARR depends only on the observed data, not on the missing values themselvesConditioning on the right observed columns removes the pattern entirely -- the working assumption behind most practical fixes
MNARR depends on the missing values themselves, even after conditioning on every observed columnCannot be verified using only the data at hand; requires domain reasoning or new data to even investigate
06
MECHANISM

Running the check, step by step

Step 1 — build the missingness indicator for the field in question: one flag per row, present or missing.
Step 2 — for each observed column you have, compute the missing rate within each of its groups.
Step 3 — flat rates across every observed column checked so far are consistent with MCAR.
Step 4 — a rate that clearly tracks one observed column, and stays flat once you condition on it, is consistent with MAR.
Step 5 — a pattern that survives conditioning on every observed column you can think of, with a plausible domain reason tied to the value itself, is grounds to suspect MNAR -- not to declare it proven.

09
MATHEMATICS

One notation, three statements

Let Xobs stand for everything observed in a row, Xmis for the value that's missing, and R for the missingness indicator itself. The three mechanisms are three different claims about how R relates to those two things.

MCAR: P(R | X_obs, X_mis) = P(R) MAR: P(R | X_obs, X_mis) = P(R | X_obs) MNAR: P(R | X_obs, X_mis) cannot be reduced to either form above
SymbolMeaning
RThe missingness indicator for one field -- 1 if missing, 0 if present, always fully observed itself
XobsEvery column actually observed for that row -- channel, product, outcome, whatever else was recorded
XmisThe value that's missing -- the one thing no column in the dataset lets you check
P(R | ...)The probability of a value being missing, conditioned on whatever sits after the bar

MCAR's right-hand side drops every condition -- missingness is just a fixed rate. MAR's right-hand side keeps Xobs but drops Xmis -- once you know the observed columns, the missing value adds nothing further. MNAR is defined by the fact that neither simplification holds.

10
EXAMPLE

Twenty complaints, a satisfaction score, and a channel that explains almost all of it

Twenty CFPB complaints, ten by phone and ten by web, each with a post-resolution satisfaction score that a follow-up survey sometimes fails to collect. Eight of the twenty are missing that score -- a plain 40% overall, the exact number Section 14's lab starts from. Split by channel: phone is missing 7 of 10, web is missing 1 of 10.

P(missing | channel = Phone) = 7/10 = 0.70 P(missing | channel = Web) = 1/10 = 0.10

A 60-percentage-point gap between two fully observed groups is not the flat signature MCAR would produce. It is, on the other hand, exactly the shape Section 05 defined as MAR: once channel is known, the gap is accounted for. Checking the same twenty rows against the true score itself -- something a real analyst never actually gets to see, but this lesson can, because it built the simulation -- shows why the channel check alone was already enough here: missing rate by score tier is 33.3% (low), 50% (mid), and 33.3% (high), a bumpy shape with no direction, not a slope tied to the value itself.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a production use, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

CFPB's satisfaction survey, split by channel

Section 10's 70% vs. 10% gap is as clean a MAR signature as real data offers -- one observed column, fully explaining the pattern.

BOUNDARY CASE

A field that's 100% missing

If a legal-review timestamp hasn't started being collected for any row yet, there's no variation in R to condition on anything -- the mechanism question doesn't even apply until some rows actually have it.

PRODUCTION USE

A conditional-missing-rate check, rerun on every new data pull

The same channel-split calculation Section 10 ran once, automated so a new mechanism showing up in fresh data gets caught immediately, not discovered months later.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Trusting a low overall rate on a field tied to the actual outcome

If satisfaction scores go missing mainly for complaints CFPB denied relief on, a 40% overall rate is hiding a mechanism concentrated exactly where Model 1 can least afford a biased label.

CODE

A conditional missing-rate check, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
rows = [
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 2}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 3},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 5}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 6},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 8}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 9},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 3}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 2},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 6}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 5},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 9}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 8},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 4}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 7},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 1}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 2},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 7}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 4},
    {"channel": "Phone", "score": 10}, {"channel": "Web", "score": 9},
]
missing_rows_mar = {1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 13, 14, 19}  # 1-indexed, matches Section 10

def missing_rate_by(rows, missing_rows, key_fn):
    totals, missing = {}, {}
    for i, row in enumerate(rows, start=1):
        key = key_fn(row)
        totals[key] = totals.get(key, 0) + 1
        if i in missing_rows:
            missing[key] = missing.get(key, 0) + 1
    return {k: round(missing.get(k, 0) / v, 3) for k, v in totals.items()}

rates = missing_rate_by(rows, missing_rows_mar, lambda r: r["channel"])
assert rates == {"Phone": 0.7, "Web": 0.1}          # matches Section 10's hand calculation

# Edge case: a group with zero rows in it has no rate to compute
def safe_rate(missing_count, total_count):
    if total_count == 0:
        return None                                  # undefined, not zero
    return missing_count / total_count

assert safe_rate(0, 0) is None

The same missing_rate_by function drives both bar groups in Section 14's lab -- once against channel, once against the (normally invisible) score tier -- just like Section 10's two-way split.

13
ECOSYSTEM

The same check, and its formal limits, in practice

df["score"].isna().groupby(df["channel"]).mean() reproduces Section 10's 0.70 / 0.10 split directly in pandas -- isna() builds the missingness indicator, groupby().mean() is the conditional rate. The missingno library turns the same idea into a matrix or heatmap across every column at once, useful for spotting a candidate observed column before you even pick one to condition on. One real statistical test exists for the MCAR side of this question -- Little's MCAR test -- but it can only reject MCAR, never confirm it, and it says nothing about whether a non-MCAR field is MAR or MNAR; that judgment call still needs the reasoning Section 06 walked through. Once a mechanism is named, sklearn.impute.MissingIndicator is the forward-looking tool that turns R itself into a feature a model can use -- Module 04's decision, flagged here only as where this concept's output eventually gets used.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

ONE-COLUMN BLINDNESS

Checking a single observed column and stopping there

A flat rate against channel doesn't rule out a real pattern against product type or outcome -- Section 06's Step 2 has to be repeated across candidates, not run once.

LOW-RATE COMPLACENCY

Assuming a small overall percentage means the mechanism can't matter

Section 11's mistake card shows how a low rate concentrated in one outcome group can still bias exactly what matters most.

INDICATOR-AS-NOISE

Discarding the missingness flag instead of keeping it as data

R is fully observed by definition -- throwing it away throws away real information about the row, not just about the missing field.

TEST OVERREACH

Treating a passed MCAR test as proof, not just a failure to reject

Little's MCAR test can reject MCAR; it cannot confirm it, and it says nothing about MAR versus MNAR either way.

17
CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
A statistical test can prove a field is MCARLittle's MCAR test can only reject the MCAR hypothesis when the data contradicts it -- passing it is a failure to reject, never a proof, and the test is silent on MAR versus MNAR.
MNAR means someone made a data-collection mistakeMNAR is often an inherent property of what's being measured -- a satisfaction survey the least satisfied customers skip isn't a bug, it's the mechanism itself.
The three mechanisms rank by how "bad" the missing rate isThey classify a reason, not a severity. A 5% MNAR field can be more dangerous than a 40% MCAR one, exactly as Section 11's mistake card shows.
18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Conditional missing rates by observed column deserve the same standing, rerun-on-every-pull treatment as any other data-quality check -- a mechanism that was MCAR last quarter can quietly become MAR after a policy or channel-mix change.
  • Every field with material missingness deserves a documented, best-guess mechanism next to it, so the next person doesn't have to re-derive Section 06's reasoning from scratch.
  • A systematically missing outcome-adjacent field is not just a data-quality nuisance for a regulator-facing system like CFPB's -- it can itself be a signal worth escalating, separate from whatever gets decided about fixing it downstream.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Section 10 found a 70% vs. 10% missing-rate gap between phone and web. Why is that consistent with MAR rather than MCAR?
Because the gap tracks channel, a column that's fully observed for every row. MCAR would require the missing rate to stay roughly flat no matter which observed column you condition on -- a 60-point swing across channel rules that out.
Why couldn't Section 10's channel check, on its own, ever detect an MNAR pattern?
Because MNAR is defined by a dependence on the missing value itself, not on any observed column. A channel-conditioned rate can be perfectly flat while the gap still tracks the hidden score directly -- checking channel only rules out channel as the explanation.
A colleague says a field is "probably fine" because only 5% of it is missing. What question would you ask back?
Missing from whom, specifically -- is that 5% spread evenly across every group you care about, or concentrated in one outcome or subgroup? Section 11's mistake card and Section 17's misconception table both show why the overall rate alone can't answer that.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open one defensible reasoning pattern
Rates: Credit reporting 2/10 = 20%, Debt collection 2/10 = 20%, Mortgage 9/10 = 90%. Mortgage stands far apart from the other two, which sit close together -- consistent with MAR, since product type (an observed column) appears to explain most of the gap. PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

The rule

  • MCAR: missingness depends on nothing -- flat against every observed column
  • MAR: missingness depends on an observed column -- flat once you condition on it
  • MNAR: missingness depends on the value itself -- never fully checkable from the data alone

Memorable example

Twenty complaints, one 40% overall missing rate, three different reasons: a flat coin flip, a 70%-vs-10% channel split, or a hidden 83%-to-0% slope by true score.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you explain the difference between MAR and MNAR to a non-technical stakeholder?
MAR is a gap you can explain using information you already have -- "night-shift workers always miss this seating." MNAR is a gap that depends on the very thing that's missing -- "people are skipping this seat because of who else is sitting there," which you'd only know by asking the unasked question.
A colleague wants to drop every row with a missing satisfaction score before training. What would you ask first?
Whether the missingness has been diagnosed at all -- if it's closer to MAR (tied to channel, as Section 10 found) or MNAR (tied to satisfaction itself), dropping those rows removes a non-random slice of the data, not a random sample, and can bias whatever gets trained on what's left.
Why can't a single statistical test settle whether a field is MCAR, MAR, or MNAR?
Little's MCAR test only tests the MCAR hypothesis, and only in the direction of rejecting it -- it has nothing to say about distinguishing MAR from MNAR, which requires reasoning about the value itself, not just a test statistic on the observed columns.
System design: how would you operationalize this concept's check across a live data pipeline?
Run a conditional-missing-rate check against every observed candidate column on each new data pull, log the result over time the way a drift monitor would, and flag any field whose rate newly diverges by group -- turning Section 06's manual reasoning into a repeatable, automated pass rather than a one-time investigation.
