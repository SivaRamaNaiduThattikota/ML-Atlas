MODULE 03 · CONCEPT 05

No single value moved.
The whole field did.

Concept 04 asked whether one value inside a snapshot sits far enough from the rest to call extreme. This concept asks a different question: has an entire field's typical range shifted between two separate snapshots of the same data — a training-time pull versus a later production pull — even if every value in both snapshots looks perfectly ordinary on its own? The two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test compares whole empirical distributions directly, and its own math reveals something counter-intuitive: the exact same shift can be flagged or missed purely because of how much data you happened to pull.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

A whole snapshot standing apart from another, not one point standing apart from its own

CONCEPT 04

Asked whether one point stands out from the rest of its own snapshot — an outlier, judged against z-score or IQR bounds.

CONCEPT 05

Asks whether one whole snapshot stands apart from another snapshot of the same field, even when every point inside each one looks perfectly ordinary.

First memory: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test compares two empirical distributions directly by finding the largest gap between their cumulative curves — one number, D, that summarizes how different two whole snapshots really are.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

A model learns "normal" from one snapshot; the world can quietly move on

A model is trained on one pull of data at one moment. If the real-world field it depends on quietly shifts afterward — typical resolution times creep up, complaint volumes change composition — the model's learned notion of "normal" can go stale even though no single value in the new data looks broken or extreme. Concept 04's tools would find nothing wrong, because they only ever look inside one snapshot at a time. This concept exists to catch exactly that blind spot: comparing a field's whole distribution across two snapshots, not just checking individual values within one.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Two eight-complaint snapshots of the same field, three days apart on average

CFPB Model 1's resolution-time field, pulled twice. Training-time pull (8 complaints): 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8 days. A later production pull (8 complaints): 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10, 11 days — every value exactly 3 days higher.

TRAINING PULL

Median around 5.5 days

3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8 — a tight, ordinary-looking spread.

PRODUCTION PULL

Median around 8.5 days

6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10, 11 — the identical spread, shifted 3 days later.

Walking both snapshots' empirical cumulative curves side by side, the largest gap between them is D = 0.625, occurring at 6 days. But at just 8 complaints per snapshot, this visually obvious 3-day shift is not statistically significant at the conventional 5% level — the sample is too small to rule out coincidence. Pull twice as much data with the identical shift, and it becomes significant. Every number here was independently verified by script.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Build two staircases, measure the biggest gap between them

STEP 1

Build each snapshot's empirical CDF

At any value x, the empirical CDF is the fraction of that snapshot's own values that are ≤ x — a staircase that climbs from 0 to 1.

STEP 2

Compare the two staircases at every step

At each distinct value across both snapshots, compute the absolute difference between the two CDFs.

STEP 3

D is the largest of those differences

Section 03's example: the gap peaks at 0.625, at 6 days.

STEP 4

Compare D against a sample-size-dependent threshold

The same D can cross or fail to cross that threshold purely because the threshold itself shrinks as sample size grows — Section 09 derives exactly why.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"An overlapping histogram that looks different proves the distribution changed."

Eyeballing two histograms is not a statistical test -- Section 03's own example looks like an obvious 3-day shift and still isn't significant at n=8.

"A non-significant KS result proves nothing changed."

It only proves the sample couldn't rule out coincidence at this size -- Section 10's scaling table shows the identical shift becoming significant at larger n, with D itself never changing at all.

"Detecting a shift is the same as fixing it."

A flagged shift is a candidate for investigation, not an automatic retrain trigger -- the same detection-is-not-a-decision boundary Concept 04 already drew for outliers.

"This is the same thing as production drift monitoring."

This concept compares two static pulls once, by hand. Module 17 runs a continuous, automated, scheduled version of a related idea -- a different mechanism, not this concept's job.

04
INTUITION

Eyeballing two histograms versus a formal ruler

Overlaying two histograms and squinting at whether they look different is like guessing a room's temperature by touch — a rough read that can mislead in either direction. The KS test is a thermometer: it builds each sample's own cumulative staircase and measures precisely how far apart the two staircases ever get, then checks that gap against a threshold that accounts for exactly how much data went into building each staircase.

Analogy boundaryThe thermometer captures "a precise measurement instead of a guess." It doesn't capture why the threshold itself shrinks as sample size grows — Section 09's algebra gives the exact reason, not just the shape of the idea.

PRECISION

Formal definitions

TermDefinitionBoundary
Empirical CDFFor a sample of size n, the function F_n(x) = (fraction of sample values ≤ x) — a staircase rising from 0 to 1Defined entirely by the sample itself, no assumed distribution shape
Two-sample KS statistic DThe largest absolute difference between two samples' empirical CDFs, over every possible xD_(n,m) = sup_x |F_n(x) − G_m(x)|
Null hypothesisBoth samples were drawn from the same underlying distributionA large D relative to the threshold is evidence against this
Asymptotic critical valueThe threshold D must exceed to be called significant at a chosen confidence level, for samples of size n and mD_crit(α) = c(α) × √((n+m)/(n·m)), c(0.05) ≈ 1.36
06
MECHANISM

Build, compare, threshold

Step 1 — sort each snapshot and build its own empirical CDF, a staircase rising by 1/n at each of its own values.
Step 2 — at every distinct value across both combined snapshots, compute the absolute difference between the two CDFs.
Step 3 — D is the largest of those differences, wherever it occurs.
Step 4 — compute the critical value D_crit for the actual sample sizes n and m, and compare D against it (or read off the equivalent p-value).
Step 5 — a shift with D above D_crit is flagged as a candidate for investigation -- not a verdict, and not a retraining decision.

09
MATHEMATICS

Why the same D can pass or fail, purely from sample size

The asymptotic critical value for the two-sample KS test at confidence level α is:

D_crit(α) = c(α) × √((n + m) / (n·m))

where c(α) is a constant depending only on the confidence level (c(0.05) ≈ 1.36), and n, m are the two sample sizes. If both samples are scaled up by the same factor k (n→kn, m→km), the ratio (n+m)/(n·m) scales by 1/k, so D_crit scales by 1/√k -- the threshold shrinks as samples grow, while a genuine, fixed underlying shift keeps producing the same D regardless of how many times the same pattern is duplicated. This is exactly why a real shift with a fixed D can start below D_crit (undetectable) and end up above it (detectable) purely by pulling more data -- nothing about the shift itself needs to change.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a production use, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

A larger pull catching a genuine seasonal shift

With hundreds of complaints per snapshot, a real seasonal or policy-driven shift in resolution time gets flagged with far more confidence than Section 10's small example allows.

BOUNDARY CASE

A statistically significant but practically trivial half-day shift

At a large enough sample size, even a tiny, operationally meaningless shift can cross D_crit — the direct Module 02 Concept 09 callback: statistical significance is not the same question as practical significance.

PRODUCTION USE

A genuine product-mix change

A new complaint category launching can legitimately raise typical resolution time for a real, explainable reason — a flagged shift here is a prompt to investigate, not evidence of a broken pipeline.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Declaring "no drift" from a KS test on a tiny pull

Section 10's own table shows a real, fixed-size shift failing to reach significance purely from too little data — absence of significance is not evidence of absence.

12
CODE

The KS statistic and critical value, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
def empirical_cdf(sample, x):
    return sum(1 for v in sample if v <= x) / len(sample)

def ks_statistic(sample_a, sample_b):
    combined = sorted(set(sample_a) | set(sample_b))
    gaps = [abs(empirical_cdf(sample_a, x) - empirical_cdf(sample_b, x)) for x in combined]
    return max(gaps)

def critical_value(n, m, c=1.36):
    return c * ((n + m) / (n * m)) ** 0.5

train = [3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8]
prod = [6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10, 11]

D = ks_statistic(train, prod)
assert round(D, 3) == 0.625

d_crit_n8 = critical_value(8, 8)
assert round(d_crit_n8, 3) == 0.680
assert D < d_crit_n8  # not significant at n=8

# Duplicate both arrays -- D must stay exactly the same, only the
# threshold changes, since the underlying shift shape is unchanged.
for k in (2, 4, 8):
    scaled_train = train * k
    scaled_prod = prod * k
    assert ks_statistic(scaled_train, scaled_prod) == D
    d_crit_k = critical_value(8 * k, 8 * k)
    if k == 2:
        assert D > d_crit_k  # significant once sample size doubles

19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why does D stay exactly 0.625 no matter how many times Section 10's two arrays get duplicated?
Duplicating both arrays the same number of times preserves the exact shape of both empirical CDFs -- every proportion stays identical, so the largest gap between the two staircases never changes. Only the sample sizes (and therefore the critical value) change.
Why did Section 10's identical shift fail significance at n=8 but pass at n=16?
The critical value D_crit scales down by 1/√k when both samples grow by a factor of k, while D itself stays fixed. At n=8, D_crit (0.68) exceeded D (0.625); at n=16, D_crit (0.481) fell below it.
A colleague says "no significant KS result, so there's no drift." What would you push back on?
That absence of significance isn't evidence of absence -- a real shift can simply be undetectable at a small sample size, exactly as Section 10's own worked example demonstrates with a genuine, fixed 3-day shift.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
Combined sorted values: 40,42,44,45,46,48,49,50,52. Walking both CDFs, the largest gap occurs at x=48: F_baseline(48)=1.0, F_recent(48)=0.5, diff=0.5 (checking neighboring points confirms this is the max). D=0.5. Using D_crit scaling by 1/√k, reaching D_crit≈0.5 requires roughly (0.785/0.5)²≈2.5, so a sample-size multiplier of about 3× (n=18 per snapshot) would be needed to bring the threshold down to where this exact D becomes significant.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

The rule

  • D: the largest gap between two samples' empirical CDFs -- a whole-distribution comparison, not a single-value check
  • D_crit: shrinks as sample size grows, by a factor of 1/√k when both samples scale by k
  • The counter-intuitive fact: the same real shift can be undetectable at a small n and clearly significant at a larger one, with D itself never changing
  • Detection ≠ decision: a flagged shift is a candidate for investigation, not an automatic retrain trigger

Memorable example

An obvious 3-day resolution-time shift: not significant at 8 complaints per snapshot (p=0.087), clearly significant at 16 (p=0.003) -- same shift, same D=0.625, different sample size.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you check whether a feature's distribution has shifted between training and production data?
Run a two-sample KS test (or an equivalent distributional comparison) between the training-time and production snapshots of that feature, compute D and its p-value at the actual sample sizes involved, and treat a flagged result as a prompt for investigation rather than an automatic action.
Why might a real, meaningful distribution shift fail to show up as statistically significant?
The sample size may simply be too small -- the critical value a KS statistic must exceed shrinks as sample size grows, so the identical shift that's undetectable at a small n can become clearly significant with more data, with no change to the shift itself.
What's the difference between this concept's approach and production drift monitoring?
This concept compares two static, already-collected snapshots once, typically by hand during exploratory analysis. Production drift monitoring runs a related comparison continuously and automatically against live data, with defined retraining triggers -- a different, later mechanism covered in Module 17.
Design question: a stakeholder wants a single dashboard number for "has this feature drifted." What would you push back on?
That a single flagged/not-flagged bit hides the sample size and p-value behind it -- the same nominal result means very different things at n=50 versus n=5,000, and collapsing that into one bit risks both false confidence at small n and false alarms at very large n on practically trivial shifts.
