MODULE 03 · CONCEPT 06

They moved together.
That's not the same as why.

Concept 02 taught you to compute Pearson's r and drew one boundary around it without proving it: correlation measures association, never causation. This concept collects on that promise. A strong correlation between two fields is consistent with several different underlying stories -- one variable causing the other, the reverse, a third factor driving both at once (confounding), or plain coincidence riding a shared trend (spurious correlation) -- and the number itself cannot tell you which. A worked CFPB example makes the trap concrete: a strong, clean-looking pooled correlation that all but vanishes, and flips sign, once you split the data by the real driver behind it.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

A number that measures agreement, not a claim about why

CONCEPT 02

Measured how strongly two variables move together -- Pearson's r, computed by hand from real CFPB-style rows.

CONCEPT 06

Asks whether that number means either variable causes the other -- and shows several other ordinary stories that produce the exact same number.

First memory: a strong correlation is consistent with A causing B, B causing A, a third factor Z driving both (confounding), or pure coincidence riding a shared trend (spurious correlation) -- and data alone often can't tell you which one you're looking at.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

EDA only sees rows that already happened, with nothing randomly assigned

Every row in an EDA dataset is something that occurred on its own, not something a researcher assigned at random the way a controlled experiment does. Nobody flipped a coin to decide which complaints would get a mortgage product type and which would get credit reporting. Because of that, any strong association you find is compatible with several different underlying mechanisms, and the raw number gives no receipt for which one produced it. Distinguishing them needs more than the correlation itself -- it needs a way to test candidate explanations against the data.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Eight complaints, two product types, one correlation that tells the wrong story

CFPB Model 1's two running fields again: resolution time (days) and satisfaction score (0-10). Credit reporting (n=4): resolution = 3, 4, 5, 6; satisfaction = 8, 9, 7, 8. Mortgage (n=4): resolution = 10, 11, 12, 13; satisfaction = 4, 3, 5, 4.

CREDIT REPORTING ONLY

r ≈ -0.3162

Resolution 3-6 days, satisfaction 7-9 -- a weak, barely-there negative relationship within this product type alone.

MORTGAGE ONLY

r ≈ +0.3162

Resolution 10-13 days, satisfaction 3-5 -- the same weak strength, but the opposite sign.

Now pool all eight rows together, the way a first glance at the whole dataset would: r ≈ -0.8981. That's a strong, clean-looking negative correlation -- exactly the kind of number that invites the claim "slower resolution causes lower satisfaction." But within each product type separately, the relationship is weak and flips sign. The pooled number isn't describing a real link between resolution time and satisfaction at all. It's almost entirely an artifact of product type -- mortgage complaints are inherently slower to resolve and inherently harder to leave satisfied, for reasons that have nothing to do with speed itself, and that shared driver pushes both variables in the same direction across the two groups. Every r above was computed with two independently coded Pearson formulas that agreed to four decimal places.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Two named traps, and one practical test for the first of them

CONFOUNDING

A third factor drives both variables

Product type independently pushes resolution time up and satisfaction down. Neither variable causes the other -- Z (product type) causes both.

SPURIOUS CORRELATION

No real mechanism at all

Two variables that each independently trend over the same shared axis -- most commonly time -- can show a high r purely from riding that shared trend, with nothing connecting them.

THE PRACTICAL TEST

Split by the suspected confounder, recompute r

Section 03's own move: split the eight rows by product type and recompute r inside each group. If it collapses or flips, the pooled number was mostly about the split variable.

THE HONEST LIMIT

Agreement doesn't prove you found THE cause

Ruling out one confounder doesn't rule out every other unmeasured one, and it doesn't rule out coincidence either -- Section 06 states this caveat in full.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"A strong r automatically means A causes B."

Section 03's own pooled r ≈ -0.898 is the counter-example -- strong, and almost entirely explained by product type instead.

"If it's not confounding, it must be causal."

This ignores spurious correlation entirely -- two variables can move together with no mechanism at all, just a shared trend.

"The correlation vanished after I controlled for one variable, so I found THE cause."

It proves confounding by that specific variable. It doesn't rule out other unmeasured confounders, and it doesn't rule out coincidence either.

"This EDA finding alone justifies launching an intervention."

An observed association, however clean, is not the same evidence as a designed experiment -- Section 05's RCT boundary exists for exactly this gap.

04
INTUITION

Two coworkers who always arrive at the same time

Two coworkers show up at the office within a minute of each other, every single day. One story: they carpool -- a real causal link, one person's timing directly affecting the other's. A different story: they both just start at 9am, and the clock is what actually drives both arrival times -- a shared external cause, a confounder, with no link between the two people's schedules at all. From the arrival-time correlation alone, you cannot tell which story is true. You'd need to ask them, or watch what happens on a day one of them works from home.

Analogy boundaryThe carpool-versus-clock story captures the idea that a third cause can drive two things at once. It doesn't capture the split-and-recompute test itself, which needs real data -- Section 06 gives the actual mechanism.

06
MECHANISM

Identify, split, recompute, compare, interpret with caution

Step 1 — identify a plausible third variable Z that could plausibly drive both X and Y (here, product type).
Step 2 — split the data into groups by the levels of Z (credit reporting rows versus mortgage rows).
Step 3 — recompute r separately within each level of Z, using only that group's own rows.
Step 4 — compare the within-level r's to the original pooled r. A collapse toward zero, or a sign flip, is evidence the pooled number was largely about Z.
Step 5 — interpret with an honest caveat: agreement doesn't rule out other unmeasured confounders, and it doesn't rule out a coincidental match either -- it only tests the one candidate you named in Step 1.

09
MATHEMATICS

Why the within-group r's never move while the pooled one does

Pooled covariance decomposes exactly into a within-group term and a between-group term (the law of total covariance):

Cov(X,Y) = (1/N)·Σ_g n_g·Cov_g(X,Y) + (1/N)·Σ_g n_g·(x̄_g − x̄)(ȳ_g − ȳ)

The first term is the size-weighted average of each group's own covariance -- it depends only on each group's internal shape, never on how far apart the groups sit. The second term depends only on the group means' distance from the overall mean, weighted by group size -- it has nothing to do with either group's internal shape. In Section 10's worked example, the within-group term comes out to exactly zero (credit's covariance is -0.25, mortgage's is +0.25, and with four rows in each group those cancel perfectly), so the entire pooled covariance of -7 is the between-group term alone -- an unusually clean illustration of the general principle. Parametrizing the two groups' mean separation by s (holding every row's within-group residual fixed) makes the between-group term work out to exactly -7s², while the within-group term stays fixed at zero for every value of s. That is the exact reason the lab's credit r and mortgage r never move as the separation slider changes, while the pooled r grows from 0 toward -1 purely as a function of how far apart the two clusters' means are pushed.

CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a production use, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Product-type confounding, exactly as computed

Section 10's own result: a strong pooled r that collapses to weak, sign-flipped numbers once split by the real driver -- a textbook confounding pattern.

BOUNDARY CASE

A real but small effect, hard to tell apart from confounding

If the within-group r's had both come out small and same-signed instead of flipped, you'd be left in a genuinely ambiguous spot -- a small true effect and residual, unmeasured confounding can look nearly identical. Not resolved here.

PRODUCTION USE

Acting on a causal claim only after a designed test

Once a candidate driver survives the split-and-recompute check, the next step toward an actual causal claim is a designed intervention or A/B test -- not another pass over the same raw EDA correlation.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Declaring causation from a strong pooled r alone

Section 03's own trap: -0.898 looks decisive and is mostly a product-type artifact.

12
CODE

Pooled r, then split-and-recompute, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
def pearson_r(x, y):
    n = len(x)
    mean_x = sum(x) / n
    mean_y = sum(y) / n
    sxy = sum((xi - mean_x) * (yi - mean_y) for xi, yi in zip(x, y))
    sxx = sum((xi - mean_x) ** 2 for xi in x)
    syy = sum((yi - mean_y) ** 2 for yi in y)
    return sxy / (sxx * syy) ** 0.5

def split_and_recompute(x, y, group):
    groups = {}
    for xi, yi, g in zip(x, y, group):
        groups.setdefault(g, ([], []))[0].append(xi)
        groups[g][1].append(yi)
    return {g: pearson_r(gx, gy) for g, (gx, gy) in groups.items()}

resolution = [3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 11, 12, 13]
satisfaction = [8, 9, 7, 8, 4, 3, 5, 4]
product = ["credit", "credit", "credit", "credit",
           "mortgage", "mortgage", "mortgage", "mortgage"]

pooled_r = pearson_r(resolution, satisfaction)
assert round(pooled_r, 4) == -0.8981

within = split_and_recompute(resolution, satisfaction, product)
assert round(within["credit"], 4) == -0.3162
assert round(within["mortgage"], 4) == 0.3162
# same eight rows, opposite conclusion once split by the confounder

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

UNMEASURED CONFOUNDER

You can't condition on data you never collected

The split-and-recompute test only ever checks the specific variable you named -- a confounder that was never in the dataset can't be tested this way at all.

REVERSE CAUSATION

Mistaking which direction the arrow points

A correlation between two variables says nothing about which one, if either, is the cause -- "longer resolution predicts lower satisfaction" is equally consistent with dissatisfaction slowing down the process itself.

COINCIDENTAL SHORT-SERIES CORRELATION

Two short trends that happen to move together

With only a handful of time points, two genuinely unrelated series riding their own separate trends can still land on a high r purely by chance.

REALITY

Production implications

  • A confounded feature can still be a perfectly good predictive feature -- correlation is sufficient for prediction, even when it is invalid grounds for a causal or intervention claim. Product type predicting satisfaction is still useful for a model, even though it doesn't mean resolution time is the lever to pull.
  • Before turning a relief-risk score into an actual policy action rather than just a ranking, CFPB needs exactly this distinction settled: is the underlying relationship causal, or just associative and good enough for ranking alone?
  • Document which candidate confounders were actually tested, and which weren't, alongside any EDA finding -- an untested confounder is a real gap, not a settled question.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why do credit r and mortgage r never move as the lab's separation slider changes?
Each group's within-group covariance depends only on that group's own residual shape, which the parametrization holds fixed at every separation level. Only the between-group term -- driven by how far apart the two group means sit -- changes, and that term is exactly what the pooled r picks up.
How do you tell confounding apart from spurious correlation?
Confounding has a real third variable causally driving both X and Y -- splitting by it and recomputing r reveals that. Spurious correlation has no such mechanism at all; it's often just two variables riding the same shared trend, like time, with nothing connecting them causally.
What's still true about a confounded feature, even though it isn't a causal lever?
It can still be a genuinely useful predictive feature -- correlation alone is sufficient grounds for prediction, even though it's invalid grounds for a causal or intervention claim.

Open the worked solution

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

The rule

  • Confounding: a real third factor Z drives both X and Y independently -- split by Z, recompute r, watch it collapse or flip
  • Spurious correlation: no mechanism at all -- often just two variables riding the same shared trend
  • Pooled vs. within-group: pooled covariance = within-group average + between-group term -- the between-group term is what a confounder inflates
  • The honest limit: ruling out one confounder never rules out every other one, or coincidence

Memorable example

At zero separation between product types, pooled r lands on exactly 0.0000, while credit r and mortgage r stay locked at -0.3162 / +0.3162 -- the same as at every other separation level. Only the gap between the groups, never either group's own shape, produced the pooled number.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you test whether a correlation you found is confounded by a specific variable?
Split the data into groups by the levels of that variable and recompute the correlation within each group. If it collapses toward zero or reverses sign compared to the pooled number, the pooled correlation was largely driven by that variable rather than a direct relationship.
What's the precise difference between confounding and spurious correlation?
Confounding involves a real third variable that causally influences both variables in question. Spurious correlation has no real causal mechanism at all -- it's typically two variables that happen to move together, often because both are trending along some shared axis like time.
When is it fine to act on a correlation without establishing causation, and when isn't it?
It's fine when the goal is prediction -- a model only needs a feature to be associated with the target, not to cause it. It isn't fine when the goal is an intervention or policy action, because acting on the wrong lever based on a confounded correlation can waste effort or actively cause harm.
Design question: a stakeholder wants to act on an EDA correlation immediately. What would you push back on?
That an observed association, however strong, hasn't ruled out confounding or coincidence, and that the evidence bar for an intervention is higher than the evidence bar for a ranking feature -- I'd ask what candidate confounders have been tested, and whether a smaller designed test could validate the causal claim before committing to the full action.
