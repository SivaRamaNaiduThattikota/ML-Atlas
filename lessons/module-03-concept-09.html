The number that already
knows what happens next.

Concept 08 asked how many distinct values a feature field holds. This concept returns to Module 01 Concept 08's leakage concept -- information that couldn't have existed at prediction time reaching training, features, or preprocessing -- and narrows it to a moment before any model exists at all: EDA. Temporal leakage in EDA means computing a summary statistic, a distribution, or a correlation using rows that are chronologically after the point in time you're claiming to analyze. An analyst pulling CFPB Model 1's resolution-time log "as of Feb 15" and averaging all ten rows on file is quietly including three complaints that hadn't happened yet as of that date -- 5.00 days honestly, 6.60 days once the future leaks in, a 32.00% inflation from nothing but a missing date filter. The fix is a four-step as-of cutoff test: pick a cutoff, split rows into known and future relative to it, compute using only known rows, and audit for silent future inclusion. This same discipline is what makes Concept 05's two-snapshot comparison honest in the first place, and it's the EDA-time counterpart to the temporal train/test splits Module 14 Concept 04 builds later.

MODULE 01 · CONCEPT 08

Information that couldn't have existed at prediction time reaching training, features, or preprocessing -- the general leakage concept.

THIS CONCEPT

The identical failure mode, narrowed to EDA: does a summary statistic, distribution, or correlation secretly depend on rows chronologically after the point being analyzed -- before any model even exists.

First memory: a CFPB resolution-time average reported "as of Feb 15" that actually includes three complaints from Feb 19 through Mar 5 -- rows that hadn't happened yet as of the date being claimed.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

EDA loads "the dataset," not "the dataset as of a date"

Exploratory analysis usually just pulls a table and starts summarizing it. Nobody pauses to ask which rows would actually have been knowable at the moment being analyzed, because the dataset already sitting on disk feels like settled, past history. It isn't -- a "Date received" field on each row is a chronological boundary, and unless it's deliberately checked against a stated cutoff, rows from after that cutoff slide into the computation without anyone noticing, exactly the way Module 01 Concept 08's leakage slides into a training pipeline without anyone noticing.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

10 complaints, one stated cutoff, two very different averages

CFPB Model 1's resolution-time log, 10 complaints received between Jan 3 and Mar 5. An analyst doing EDA "as of Feb 15" wants to report the average resolution time so far.

KNOWN AS OF FEB 15 — 7 ROWS

Jan 3 through Feb 12

4, 5, 4, 6, 5, 6, 5 days. Sum 35, mean = 35/7 = 5.00 days -- the honest answer.

FUTURE RELATIVE TO FEB 15 — 3 ROWS

Feb 19, Feb 26, Mar 5

9, 10, 12 days. These three rows hadn't happened yet as of Feb 15 and have no business in an "as of Feb 15" average.

Mix all 10 rows in anyway and the mean becomes 66/10 = 6.60 days -- a 1.60-day, 32.00% inflation, purely from three rows that were chronologically in the future relative to the date the report claims to describe.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Four steps: cutoff, split, compute, audit

1 · PICK A CUTOFF

State the as-of date

Feb 15 in the worked example -- the date the report claims to describe.

2 · SPLIT KNOWN / FUTURE

Every row, one comparison

Date received ≤ cutoff is known (7 rows); date received > cutoff is future (3 rows).

3 · COMPUTE ON KNOWN ONLY

5.00 days, not 6.60

Any statistic -- mean, distribution, correlation -- gets computed using only the known rows.

4 · AUDIT FOR SILENT INCLUSION

Does the "final" number match?

If a reported figure doesn't match the known-only computation, future rows leaked in somewhere upstream.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"The whole dataset is 'past' data since it was already collected."

Already collected does not mean already knowable as of the date you're claiming to analyze -- Feb 19's row was collected before you ran the report, but it wasn't knowable as of Feb 15.

"Correlation and summary stats aren't training, so leakage rules don't apply."

Directly false -- this concept's whole point. Module 01 Concept 08's leakage rule applies to any number reaching any stage, including a mean computed during EDA with no model in sight.

"As long as I don't leak into the model, EDA is safe."

An EDA conclusion built on leaked rows can still misdirect a feature-engineering decision long before a model ever gets trained on anything.

Cutoff, split, compute, audit

Step 1 — pick an as-of cutoff date (Feb 15 in the worked example).
Step 2 — split every row into known (date ≤ cutoff) or future (date > cutoff) -- 7 known, 3 future.

Step 4 — audit any "final" reported number against Step 3's result -- if it doesn't match, future rows leaked in somewhere upstream.

09
MATHEMATICS

The leak as a weighted pull toward the future segment's mean

Let N be the total row count, n_honest the count of known rows, n_future the count of future rows (N = n_honest + n_future), and mean_honest / mean_future the means of each group. The naive full-dataset mean is a weighted average of the two groups:

naive_mean = (n_honest · mean_honest + n_future · mean_future) / N

Subtracting mean_honest from both sides and simplifying (using N = n_honest + n_future) isolates the bias as a term that depends only on the future segment's share of the data and how far its mean sits from the honest mean:

bias = naive_mean − mean_honest = (n_future / N) · (mean_future − mean_honest)

In the worked example: n_future = 3, N = 10, mean_future = (9+10+12)/3 = 10.3333, mean_honest = 5.00, so bias = (3/10) · (10.3333 − 5.00) = 0.3 × 5.3333 = 1.60 -- matching the direct 6.60 − 5.00 computation exactly. The formula's reading is the important part: the leak isn't a random error, it's a systematic pull toward whatever the future segment's mean happens to be, in direct proportion to how much of the future got mixed in.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a production use, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

A single fixed EDA snapshot, correctly bounded

Every row dated at or before the snapshot's own stated pull date -- no future rows exist to leak in, so honest and naive computations are identical by construction.

BOUNDARY CASE

A row dated exactly on the cutoff itself

Date received equal to the cutoff counts as known, not future -- this concept defines the boundary as inclusive, explicitly, rather than leaving it ambiguous.

PRODUCTION USE

CFPB's weekly pull, re-filtered every time

Every recurring EDA number needs the as-of cutoff re-applied to that week's own pull date -- fixing it once and reusing it later silently reintroduces the leak.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

A "current" correlation that isn't

Reporting a correlation that silently includes next week's not-yet-resolved complaints -- the relationship can look strong purely because future rows were mixed in.

CODE

An as-of filter, and the naive mean it's meant to prevent

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
from datetime import date

def as_of_filter(rows, cutoff_date):
    known = [r for r in rows if r["date"] <= cutoff_date]
    if not known:
        raise ValueError("no rows are known as of this cutoff -- check the date")
    return known

def mean(rows):
    return sum(r["value"] for r in rows) / len(rows)

rows = [
    {"date": date(2024, 1, 3),  "value": 4},
    {"date": date(2024, 1, 9),  "value": 5},
    {"date": date(2024, 1, 15), "value": 4},
    {"date": date(2024, 1, 22), "value": 6},
    {"date": date(2024, 1, 29), "value": 5},
    {"date": date(2024, 2, 5),  "value": 6},
    {"date": date(2024, 2, 12), "value": 5},
    {"date": date(2024, 2, 19), "value": 9},
    {"date": date(2024, 2, 26), "value": 10},
    {"date": date(2024, 3, 5),  "value": 12},
]
cutoff = date(2024, 2, 15)

honest_mean = mean(as_of_filter(rows, cutoff))
naive_mean = mean(rows)  # the leak: no date filter at all

assert round(honest_mean, 2) == 5.00
assert round(naive_mean, 2) == 6.60

# edge case: a cutoff before the very first row leaves nothing known --
# raise loudly rather than silently returning a NaN from an empty mean
try:
    as_of_filter(rows, date(2023, 12, 1))
    assert False, "should have raised"
except ValueError:
    pass

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • A recurring EDA report -- a weekly CFPB pull, say -- needs the as-of cutoff re-applied every single time it runs, not fixed once and reused on every later pull.
  • A leaked EDA finding can survive into a written report or a stakeholder deck long before any model or monitoring system would ever have a chance to catch it -- there's no automated gate at EDA time the way there might be later in a pipeline.
  • The audit step (Section 06, Step 4) is cheap to run habitually and expensive to skip -- re-checking a "final" number against a known-only recomputation takes seconds and catches the leak immediately.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why doesn't "the data was already collected" mean it was knowable as of the date a report claims to describe?
Collection time and knowability-as-of-a-date are different things. A row can sit on disk today while still carrying a date field that postdates the cutoff a report is claiming to describe -- it was collected before the report ran, but it wasn't knowable as of the date the report is honest about.
How does this concept differ from Module 01 Concept 08's general leakage?
Same mechanism -- information that couldn't have existed at the relevant point in time reaching a stage of the pipeline. This concept narrows it to a statistic, distribution, or correlation computed during EDA, before any model, features, or training exist at all.
Why does Concept 05's distribution-shift comparison depend on this concept?
Concept 05 assumes both of its snapshots are honestly bounded to their own pull dates. If either snapshot silently included rows after its own cutoff, the two empirical CDFs Concept 05 compares -- and the KS statistic D computed from them -- would already be wrong before the comparison even starts.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
Known as of Jan 28: [8,7,8,6], n=4, sum=29, honest mean = 29/4 = 7.25. Full dataset (naive): all 8 rows, sum=45, naive mean = 45/8 = 5.625. Future rows: [7,4,3,2], mean_future = 16/4 = 4.00. CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why does EDA need the same leakage discipline as model training?
Because leakage is about whether information that couldn't have existed at the relevant point in time reached a stage of the pipeline -- and EDA is a stage. A statistic, distribution, or correlation computed at EDA time can be just as contaminated by future rows as a training feature can, even with no model anywhere in sight.
Math: derive the leakage-bias formula and apply it to a case where 20% of rows are future with a future mean 10 points above the honest mean.
naive_mean = (n_honest·mean_honest + n_future·mean_future)/N, so bias = naive_mean − mean_honest = (n_future/N)·(mean_future − mean_honest). With n_future/N = 0.20 and (mean_future − mean_honest) = 10, bias = 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 -- the naive statistic sits 2.0 points above the honest one.
Coding: write a function that filters rows to only those knowable as of a given cutoff date.
Filter to rows where the date field is less than or equal to the cutoff, and raise explicitly rather than silently returning an empty or NaN result if no rows qualify -- Section 12's as_of_filter is exactly this function.
Debugging: a recurring EDA report's numbers don't match after re-pulling the same data source a week later. What would you check first?
Whether the as-of cutoff was re-applied to the new pull's own date, or whether the report is silently reusing last week's cutoff logic against this week's larger dataset -- the exact failure mode Section 18's production note names.
System design: how would you enforce this automatically in a recurring EDA pipeline?
Parameterize every date-bounded query by the pipeline's own run date rather than a hardcoded value, and add an automated check that recomputes at least one key statistic on the known-only subset and asserts it matches the reported figure -- turning Section 06's manual Step 4 audit into a build-time gate.
