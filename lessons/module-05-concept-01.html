MODULE 05 · CONCEPT 01

Diagnosed the skew.
Now: what does training actually do about it?

Module 03 Concept 07 already tallied the damage: CFPB's real relief-granted split is 223 minority rows against 777 majority rows, a 3.48:1 ratio, "mild." This concept doesn't re-measure that skew -- it explains the mechanism that turns a skewed label into a model that learns almost nothing about the rare class. An unweighted training objective minimizes total error, full stop, and a lopsided split hands it a cheap way to do that: predict the majority class for everyone, and total error already sits at just 223 -- a floor that costs nothing to reach and teaches the model nothing about the minority class it never bothered to look at. The exact condition for a real model to actually beat that floor turns out to be a clean identity, and Module 02's own Model B -- which tied the floor rather than beating it -- sits precisely on the boundary line that identity draws.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Diagnosis vs. mechanism

MODULE 03 CONCEPT 07

Diagnosed HOW skewed the target is: 223 relief-granted vs. 777 non-relief, ratio 3.48:1, severity "mild."

THIS CONCEPT

Explains WHY that skew actually breaks a model trained with no imbalance-aware adjustment: it minimizes total error, and a lopsided split lets it do that by simply ignoring the rare class.

First memory: naive training doesn't fight the imbalance -- it exploits it.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

The objective treats every row's error identically

A plain training objective (unweighted 0/1 loss, or its smooth stand-ins like log-loss) treats every row's error the same regardless of class. With 777 majority rows and 223 minority rows, predicting majority-for-everyone already produces only 223 errors -- a floor a model can sit on without learning anything about the rare class. Nothing in the objective itself objects to that.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Two constant rules, and a 3.48× free lunch

CFPB's real 223/777 split (cited from Concept 07, not re-tallied). Always predict majority: 223 errors, 77.7% accuracy. Always predict minority: 777 errors, 22.3% accuracy. The majority-only rule wins by construction, with zero learned signal, purely because there are fewer minority rows to get wrong -- a 3.48× "free lunch" the class split itself hands to a naive objective for doing nothing.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

ΔE = TP − FP, the exact condition for beating the floor

Since caught-plus-missed minority rows always sum to 223, total error simplifies to 223 − (TP − FP). A model only beats the naive majority-only floor if TP > FP -- strictly more newly-caught real relief cases than newly-created false alarms. Module 02's own Model B (TP=100, FP=100) makes ΔE exactly 0 -- it ties the floor rather than beating it, same accuracy, different composition.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"If training accuracy is high, the model learned something real."

False -- it could be sitting entirely on the free majority-only floor.

"Any real signal in a feature is enough to help."

False -- it must clear the break-even lift (more than 3.48× in CFPB's case), a much higher bar than "better than nothing."

"This is the same as Concept 07's diagnosis."

False -- Concept 07 measured the skew; this concept explains what training does in response to it.

04
INTUITION

Grading total mistakes across two very different-sized teams

A manager grading total mistakes across a 777-person team and a 223-person team can cut corners on the small team almost for free -- it barely moves the total tally. A naive training objective faces exactly the same incentive: ignoring the small class costs little against the total error count.

Analogy boundaryThe team-grading picture explains the incentive. It doesn't state the exact ΔE algebra -- Section 09 gives that precisely.

09
MATHEMATICS

Deriving ΔE and the break-even lift

TP + FN = n_minority (always) ⟹ error = FP + FN = FP + (n_minority − TP) = n_minority − (TP − FP)
ΔE = TP − FP
Break-even: TP > FP ⟺ rTP/rFP > n_majority/n_minority

The second line follows from dividing TP by n_minority and FP by n_majority to get rates: TP > FP ⟺ rTP·n_minority > rFP·n_majority ⟺ rTP/rFP > n_majority/n_minority.

10
WORKED EXAMPLE

Two floors, one identity, one boundary check

CFPB's real split (cited, Module 03 Concept 07): n_minority=223, n_majority=777, N=1,000, ratio=777/223=3.4843.

RuleErrorAccuracy
Always predict majority22377.70%
Always predict minority77722.30%

Module 02's Model A (TP=0, FP=0, FN=223, TN=777) is exactly the majority-only floor. Model B (TP=100, FP=100, FN=123, TN=677): ΔE = 100−100 = 0 -- ties the floor exactly, same 77.70% accuracy, different composition. Its capture rate rTP=100/223=0.4484 and false-flag rate rFP=100/777=0.1287; the ratio rTP/rFP = 3.4843, exactly equal to n_majority/n_minority -- Model B sits precisely on the break-even boundary, which is why it ties rather than beats or loses to the floor.

A "plausible-sounding trade that still fails": rTP=10%, rFP=3% → TP=22.30, FP=23.31, ΔE=−1.01, new error ≈224.01, new accuracy ≈77.60% -- slightly WORSE than the 77.70% floor, even though 10% catch vs. 3% false-flag sounds clearly favorable. The break-even rTP needed at rFP=3% is 3%×3.4843=10.45% -- 10% falls just short.

VERIFICATION: every number above was produced by an executed Node.js script, run twice (once for the floor/identity/Model-B checks, once for the ratio and break-even-lift table), with every intermediate value cross-checked against the algebraic identities (ΔE=TP−FP; ratio identity rTP/rFP=(TP/FP)×(n_majority/n_minority), collapsing to exactly n_majority/n_minority when TP=FP).

CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

The two constant-rule floors

Cleanly derived, no ambiguity about which one a naive objective prefers.

BOUNDARY CASE

Model B sitting exactly on the break-even line

TP=FP=100 -- ties, doesn't beat or lose to, the naive floor.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Assuming any training accuracy above the floor means real minority learning

It could still be sitting entirely on -- or even slightly below -- the free floor.

12
CODE

ΔE and the break-even check, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
def delta_e(tp, fp):
    return tp - fp

def break_even_lift(n_majority, n_minority):
    return n_majority / n_minority

def beats_floor(rtp, rfp, n_minority, n_majority):
    tp = rtp * n_minority
    fp = rfp * n_majority
    return delta_e(tp, fp) > 0

# Reproduces this concept's own Section 10 result
assert delta_e(100, 100) == 0                     # Model B ties the floor exactly
assert delta_e(100, 50) == 50                      # Model C beats it, error 173, acc 0.827
assert round(break_even_lift(777, 223), 4) == 3.4843
assert beats_floor(0.10, 0.03, 223, 777) is False  # the "plausible but fails" trade
assert beats_floor(0.1045, 0.03, 223, 777) is True # just past the break-even line

REALITY

Production implications

  • A retrained model can silently regress toward the majority-only floor if new data shifts the effective signal strength below the break-even lift, even with no code change.
  • Document the diagnosed imbalance ratio (Module 03 Concept 07) alongside any model's minority recall, not just its accuracy.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why does a naive objective default toward predicting the majority class under skew?
It minimizes total error, and the majority-only rule already achieves a low total error (223) purely because there are fewer minority rows to get wrong -- a floor reachable with zero learned signal.
What is the break-even lift, and why is it higher than "some real signal"?
rTP/rFP must exceed n_majority/n_minority (3.4843 for CFPB) -- a capture-rate advantage several times larger than the false-flag rate, not merely a positive signal.
Why does Model B tie the floor rather than beat or lose to it?
TP=FP=100 makes ΔE=TP−FP=0 exactly -- the identical accuracy as the floor, with a different (but net-zero) error composition.

TRANSFER

Hands-on exercise

A fresh CFPB narrative-tag scenario: 225 debt-collection complaints tagged either "improper contact" (majority, 180 rows) or "wrong amount" (minority, 45 rows) -- ratio 180/45 = 4.00. A candidate rule captures 8% of the "wrong amount" rows (rTP) at a false-flag rate of 1.5% on "improper contact" rows (rFP). Does it beat the naive majority-only floor?

21
FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
TP = 0.08 × 45 = 3.6. FP = 0.015 × 180 = 2.7. ΔE = 3.6 − 2.7 = 0.9, which is positive -- the rule BEATS the naive floor. New error = 45 − 0.9 = 44.1 (vs. the floor's 45), new accuracy = (225−44.1)/225 = 80.40% (vs. the floor's 80.00%). The break-even rTP at rFP=1.5% is 1.5%×4.00 = 6.00% -- the candidate's 8% clears that bar comfortably, unlike Section 10's own 10%-vs-3% example, which fell just short of its own 10.45% bar.

Teach-back: explain why an 8%-vs-1.5% trade clears its break-even bar here, while Section 10's 10%-vs-3% trade did not clear its own bar, even though both pairs of numbers "sound" similarly favorable at a glance.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why do naive classifiers default to majority-class prediction under imbalance?
An unweighted objective minimizes total error treating every row identically; under a skewed split, predicting the majority class for everyone already achieves a low total error for free, with no learning required.
Mathematical: state the empirical risk minimization framing and derive ΔE.
ERM minimizes the average/total loss over all training rows equally. Since TP+FN=n_minority always, error=FP+FN=n_minority−(TP−FP), so any error reduction below the majority-only floor is exactly ΔE=TP−FP.
Coding: implement a break-even check given capture rate, false-flag rate, and class counts.
Compute TP=rtp×n_minority, FP=rfp×n_majority, and check whether TP−FP>0 -- Section 12 gives the full from-scratch implementation with matching asserts.
Debugging: a model shows 78% accuracy on a 3.5:1 imbalanced set. What's the first thing to check?
Whether that accuracy is at or below the majority-only floor (77.7% in CFPB's case) -- if so, the model may have learned little to nothing about the minority class despite the seemingly healthy accuracy number. Check minority recall directly.
System design: how would you monitor for silent regression toward the naive floor in production?
Track minority-class recall (not just accuracy) alongside the diagnosed imbalance ratio over time -- a retrained model whose effective signal strength drops below the break-even lift will regress toward the floor with no code change and no accuracy alarm.
