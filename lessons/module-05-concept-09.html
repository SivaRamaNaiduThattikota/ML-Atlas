MODULE 05 · CONCEPT 09

One rate.
Four real ones.

Every earlier concept in this module ran its formulas against one pooled number, 223 of 777 relief-granted, 22.3%, as if it belonged to one uniform population. It doesn't -- it's four CFPB products stacked into a single figure: credit_reporting at 15.0%, debt_collection at 19.0%, mortgage at 38.0%, and credit_card at 30.0%, summing exactly back to the pooled 223/777. Recomputing Concept 03's balanced-weight formula and Concept 05's cost-derived threshold on each product separately exposes two axes that move independently: mortgage's own weight ratio, 1.632, is actually the LOWEST of the four despite having the HIGHEST relief rate, while its cost-derived threshold drops to 0.0588 -- driven not by that relief rate at all, but by a genuinely larger real cost of missing a case, C(FN)=$800. The central point this concept exists to make: t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)) contains no class-count term whatsoever, so a product's relief-rate divergence alone never justifies a different threshold -- only a real divergence in dollar costs does. Applying one global threshold anyway costs mortgage $750 today (a 4.75x multiple) by under-flagging it, and costs debt_collection $200 (a 5x multiple) by over-flagging it -- one frozen number, two opposite mistakes.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

One pooled rate, or four real ones?

CONCEPTS 01-08

Ran every formula against a single pooled number -- 223 of 777 relief-granted, 22.3% -- as if it described one uniform population.

THIS CONCEPT

Splits that same population into CFPB's four real products and asks whether one threshold and one weight ratio still fit all four.

First memory: a national weather average tells you nothing about which city is snowing and which is 90°F -- splitting by product is how you find out which is which.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

A pooled rate can hide four very different rates

The module-wide 223/777=22.3% relief rate is real, but it is an average over four products with very different underlying rates -- from 15.0% to 38.0%. Any formula run once against the pooled number silently assumes those four rates are close enough to treat as one. This concept checks that assumption by recomputing Concept 03's and Concept 05's formulas separately, per product, on genuinely different inputs.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Mortgage: 38.0% relief, but the LOWEST weight ratio

Mortgage's relief rate, 38.0%, is the furthest from the pooled 22.3% of any product -- yet its balanced-weight ratio, 1.632, is the LOWEST of the four, because a higher relief rate means a more balanced product, not a more skewed one. What actually sets mortgage apart is its real cost matrix: missing a genuine mortgage relief case is assumed to cost $800, not the global $500, because the regulatory stakes of a missed case are larger. That alone -- not the relief rate -- pulls mortgage's cost-derived threshold down to 0.0588, well below the global 0.0909.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Split the pool, then re-run two formulas per subset

Split the pooled 1,000-row set into its four real products (400/300/200/100 rows). Re-run Concept 03's balanced-weight formula, w_c=n/(2·n_c), on each product's own (n_minority, n_majority) -- this axis moves purely with each product's own relief rate. Separately, re-run Concept 05's cost-derived threshold, t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)), on each product's own real cost matrix -- this axis moves purely with each product's own dollar costs. Nothing forces these two axes to move together, and mortgage is the proof: its weight ratio is the module's lowest while its threshold is the module's lowest too, for two completely unrelated reasons.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"A different relief rate always means a different threshold is needed."

False -- t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)) has no relief-rate term at all. Only a real difference in dollar costs justifies a different threshold.

"The product with the biggest weight-ratio gap is the one that matters most."

False -- debt_collection's ratio gap from the global fix is bigger than mortgage's, yet mortgage's real cost-driven stakes are the bigger deal.

"Splitting by product is the same thing Concept 06's r-sweep did."

False -- the r-sweep varied an evaluation-time class ratio on one population; this concept splits the population itself into four real subgroups.

The weight ratio depends only on a product's own (n_minority, n_majority); the threshold depends only on a product's own (C(FP), C(FN)) -- the two never have to move together.

04
INTUITION

A national weather average tells you nothing about which city is snowing

A national average temperature of 60°F could mean every city is a mild 60°F, or it could mean one city is at 90°F and another is snowing at 30°F -- the average alone can't tell you which. CFPB's pooled 22.3% relief rate is the same kind of number: it is real, but it says nothing about which of the four products is near that average and which is far from it, or why.

Analogy boundaryThe weather picture explains why a pooled average can hide real subgroup divergence. It doesn't capture the exact arithmetic that separates a relief-rate difference from a real-cost difference -- Sections 09/10 give that precisely.

09
MATHEMATICS

t* has no n_c term -- two axes that move independently

w_c = n / (2·n_c) -- a pure function of class counts (n_minority, n_majority)
t* = C(FP) / (C(FP)+C(FN)) -- a pure function of dollar costs (C(FP), C(FN))

Neither equation shares a variable with the other. w_c depends only on how many rows are in each class; t* depends only on what a false positive or false negative actually costs in dollars. By inspection, t* contains no n_c term at all -- so no matter how far a product's relief rate drifts from 22.3%, that fact alone cannot move t*. Only a genuine change in (C(FP), C(FN)) can. This is the single mathematical fact the rest of this concept exists to demonstrate concretely.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Four products, two formulas, two opposite mistakes

Step 1 -- the per-product breakdown. The pooled 223/777 splits exactly into four real CFPB products:

Productn_minority (relief)n_majority (no relief)nRelief rate
credit_reporting6034040015.0%
debt_collection5724330019.0%
mortgage7612420038.0%
credit_card307010030.0%
TOTAL2237771,00022.3%

Mortgage is the extreme case: 38.0% versus the 22.3% pooled figure, a 1.7x divergence.

Step 2 -- Concept 03's balanced-weight formula, w_c=n/(2·n_c), recomputed per product. The global figure (223/777) reproduces Concept 03's own cited numbers exactly: w_min=2.242152466367713, w_maj=0.6435006435006435, ratio=3.4843049327354256. Recomputed fresh on each product's own counts:

  • credit_reporting (60/340): w_min=3.3333333333333335, w_maj=0.5882352941176471, ratio=5.666666666666667
  • debt_collection (57/243): w_min=2.6315789473684212, w_maj=0.6172839506172839, ratio=4.263157894736842
  • mortgage (76/124): w_min=1.3157894736842106, w_maj=0.8064516129032258, ratio=1.6315789473684212

Every ratio checks against Concept 01's own break-even identity, ratio=n_majority/n_minority -- e.g. mortgage: 124/76=1.631578947368421, matching to float precision. Note the direction: mortgage has the HIGHEST relief rate (38.0%) but the LOWEST weight ratio of the three shown -- a higher relief rate means a more balanced product, which is exactly what a smaller ratio should reflect.

Step 3 -- Concept 05's cost-derived threshold, t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)), on genuinely different per-product cost matrices. The global cost matrix (C(FN)=$500, C(FP)=$50, unchanged from Concept 05) gives t*=50/550=1/11≈0.0909. Mortgage's own cost matrix (C(FN)=$800 -- a missed relief case carries larger regulatory stakes; C(FP)=$50 unchanged) gives t*=50/850=0.058823529411765, lower and more sensitive than global. Debt-collection's own cost matrix (C(FN)=$200 -- smaller stakes than the global default; C(FP)=$50 unchanged) gives t*=50/250=0.2, higher and less sensitive than global.

The central synthesis point. t* has no n_c term at all -- it is a pure function of dollar costs. Mortgage's high relief rate (38.0%) is NOT, by itself, the reason its threshold should move; it is mortgage's real cost structure, assumed larger because the stakes of a missed case are larger, that justifies a product-specific threshold. This is the sharp distinction from the weight-ratio axis, which DOES move purely from n_c and would diverge even if every product's real costs were identical.

Step 4 -- the concrete cost of applying ONE global threshold anyway. On two fixed 10-row illustrative samples, scored under each product's own true cost matrix:

Mortgage sample -- (0.85,1), (0.60,1), (0.35,1), (0.075,1), (0.50,0), (0.20,0), (0.10,0), (0.07,0), (0.04,0), (0.02,0). At the GLOBAL threshold (0.0909), under mortgage's own $800/$50 costs: TP=3, FP=3, FN=1, TN=3 → cost=1×800+3×50=$950. At MORTGAGE'S OWN threshold (0.058824): TP=4, FP=4, FN=0, TN=2 → cost=4×50=$200. Gap=$750, a 4.75x multiple -- the global threshold under-flags mortgage's own most costly case, the row at 0.075, a real relief case worth $800 if missed, which sits between the two thresholds and is only caught by mortgage's own lower bar.

Debt-collection sample -- (0.90,1), (0.55,1), (0.25,1), (0.30,0), (0.18,0), (0.15,0), (0.12,0), (0.10,0), (0.05,0), (0.02,0). At the GLOBAL threshold (0.0909), under debt-collection's own $200/$50 costs: TP=3, FP=5, FN=0, TN=2 → cost=5×50=$250. At DEBT-COLLECTION'S OWN threshold (0.2): TP=3, FP=1, FN=0, TN=6 → cost=1×50=$50. Gap=$200, a 5x multiple -- the OPPOSITE direction from mortgage: the global threshold over-flags debt-collection with false positives that aren't worth their lower real cost.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Mortgage genuinely needs its own rule

Both axes diverge, and the cost divergence is grounded in a real, stated reason -- larger regulatory stakes on a missed case.

BOUNDARY CASE

credit_reporting's ratio diverges a lot, its threshold doesn't

Ratio 5.667 vs. global 3.484 -- a big gap. But with no evidence its real costs differ from $500/$50, its threshold correctly stays at 0.0909: weight divergence without threshold divergence.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Assuming the bigger ratio gap means the bigger stakes

debt_collection's ratio gap from global (4.263 vs. 3.484) is smaller than credit_reporting's, yet mortgage's dollar-cost gap -- not any ratio gap -- is the one grounded in real regulatory stakes.

CODE

The per-product table and both cost comparisons, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
products = {
    "credit_reporting": {"n_min": 60,  "n_maj": 340},
    "debt_collection":  {"n_min": 57,  "n_maj": 243},
    "mortgage":         {"n_min": 76,  "n_maj": 124},
    "credit_card":      {"n_min": 30,  "n_maj": 70},
}

assert sum(p["n_min"] for p in products.values()) == 223
assert sum(p["n_maj"] for p in products.values()) == 777

def weight_stats(n_min, n_maj):
    n = n_min + n_maj
    w_min = n / (2 * n_min)
    w_maj = n / (2 * n_maj)
    return w_min, w_maj, w_min / w_maj

w_min, w_maj, ratio = weight_stats(223, 777)
assert (w_min, w_maj) == (2.242152466367713, 0.6435006435006435)
assert ratio == 3.4843049327354256

mortgage_ratio = weight_stats(76, 124)[2]
assert round(mortgage_ratio, 10) == 1.6315789473684212
assert mortgage_ratio < ratio  # highest relief rate, lowest ratio

def t_star(c_fp, c_fn):
    return c_fp / (c_fp + c_fn)

assert round(t_star(50, 500), 4) == 0.0909
assert round(t_star(50, 800), 6) == 0.058824   # mortgage's own cost matrix
assert t_star(50, 200) == 0.2                  # debt collection's own cost matrix

def confusion_and_cost(rows, threshold, c_fn, c_fp):
    tp = fp = fn = tn = 0
    for score, y in rows:
        pred = 1 if score >= threshold else 0
        if pred == 1 and y == 1: tp += 1
        elif pred == 1 and y == 0: fp += 1
        elif pred == 0 and y == 1: fn += 1
        else: tn += 1
    return tp, fp, fn, tn, fn * c_fn + fp * c_fp

mortgage_rows = [(0.85,1),(0.60,1),(0.35,1),(0.075,1),
                 (0.50,0),(0.20,0),(0.10,0),(0.07,0),(0.04,0),(0.02,0)]
global_on_mortgage = confusion_and_cost(mortgage_rows, 1/11, 800, 50)
own_on_mortgage = confusion_and_cost(mortgage_rows, 50/850, 800, 50)
assert global_on_mortgage == (3, 3, 1, 3, 950)
assert own_on_mortgage == (4, 4, 0, 2, 200)
assert global_on_mortgage[-1] - own_on_mortgage[-1] == 750

debt_rows = [(0.90,1),(0.55,1),(0.25,1),
             (0.30,0),(0.18,0),(0.15,0),(0.12,0),(0.10,0),(0.05,0),(0.02,0)]
global_on_debt = confusion_and_cost(debt_rows, 1/11, 200, 50)
own_on_debt = confusion_and_cost(debt_rows, 0.2, 200, 50)
assert global_on_debt == (3, 5, 0, 2, 250)
assert own_on_debt == (3, 1, 0, 6, 50)
assert global_on_debt[-1] - own_on_debt[-1] == 200

REALITY

Production implications

  • Assign a named owner to every product-specific cost matrix, not just to the model as a whole -- mortgage's $800 C(FN) needs its own documented justification, separate from the global default.
  • Watch the proliferation risk: N product-specific rules each need Concept 08's own staleness review separately -- a per-product fix is not a one-time cost, it multiplies the maintenance surface by N.

19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why doesn't mortgage's 38.0% relief rate, by itself, justify moving its threshold?
t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)) has no term for relief rate or class counts at all. Mortgage's threshold moves only because its real cost of a missed case, C(FN)=$800, genuinely differs from the global $500 -- the relief rate is a coincidence, not the cause.
Why does the global threshold fail in opposite directions on mortgage versus debt collection?
Mortgage's real threshold is LOWER than global (0.0588 vs. 0.0909) because its real C(FN) is higher, so the frozen global threshold under-flags it. Debt collection's real threshold is HIGHER than global (0.2 vs. 0.0909) because its real C(FN) is lower, so the frozen global threshold over-flags it -- the same frozen number, two opposite real-cost mismatches.
Why is credit_reporting's large weight-ratio gap not, by itself, evidence its threshold should move?
The weight-ratio gap is driven entirely by credit_reporting's own relief rate versus the pooled 22.3% -- a base-rate fact. Its threshold only moves if its real dollar costs differ from $500/$50, which the ratio gap says nothing about.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

Two independent axes

  • Base-rate axis: the weight ratio, w_c=n/(2·n_c) -- moves only with a product's own class counts
  • Cost-structure axis: the threshold, t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)) -- moves only with a product's own dollar costs
  • The rule: relief-rate divergence alone never justifies a threshold change; only real cost divergence does

Memorable example

Mortgage's relief rate is highest (38.0%) but its weight ratio is lowest (1.632); its real $800 cost of a missed case is what actually earns it a lower threshold (0.0588) -- one frozen global number under-flags it by $750, over-flags debt_collection by $200 in the opposite direction.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why doesn't relief-rate divergence alone justify a threshold change?
The cost-optimal threshold t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)) is a function of dollar costs only. A product's relief rate can differ from the pooled average for reasons entirely unrelated to its real costs, so relief-rate divergence carries no information about whether the threshold should move.
Mathematical: show that t* contains no n_c term.
t*=C(FP)/(C(FP)+C(FN)) is defined purely in terms of C(FP) and C(FN); no n_minority or n_majority term appears anywhere in the formula, by inspection. Contrast with w_c=n/(2·n_c), which is defined purely in terms of class counts and contains no cost term -- the two formulas partition the inputs cleanly, with no shared variable.
Coding: implement per-product weight and threshold recomputation.
Section 12 gives the full implementation: a weight_stats function taking (n_min, n_maj) and a t_star function taking (c_fp, c_fn), each called once per product with that product's own inputs, plus asserts reproducing this concept's own mortgage and debt_collection numbers.
Debugging: a team applies one global threshold to all four products and gets pushback from both the mortgage and debt-collection teams. Diagnose.
The global threshold is stale in opposite directions for each: it's too low for debt_collection's real costs (over-flagging, wasting $200 per the fixed sample) and too high for mortgage's real costs (under-flagging, missing $750 worth of genuine relief cases). Both point to the same root cause -- the deployed threshold was never split by product's own real cost matrix.
System design: how would you decide whether per-product rules are worth the added ownership overhead?
Check whether a product's real dollar costs (not just its relief rate) genuinely diverge from the global default, backed by a stated business or regulatory reason. If they do -- as with mortgage's larger missed-case stakes -- a dedicated rule is worth Concept 08's added staleness-review burden. If they don't -- as with credit_reporting's large but unexplained ratio gap -- the single global rule remains the simpler, correct choice.
