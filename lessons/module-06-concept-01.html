MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 01

Draw a line.
Count what it misses.

Every earlier module measured, resampled, weighted or thresholded something that was already there. This concept builds the first thing that wasn't: an actual model. On a fresh 6-row CFPB pair -- prior_contact_attempts (x) against case_age_days (y) -- a candidate line, ŷ=2+3x, is found by a quick two-point shortcut, then scored the only way a line can be scored: predict each point, subtract to get a residual, square it, add it up. That sum, SSE=15, beats the mean-only baseline's SSE≈157.33 by a factor of roughly 10.49x. But this candidate line's six residuals don't cancel to zero -- an observation, not yet an explanation. That gap is exactly what Concept 02, named forward only, exists to close.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Correlation says they move together. Regression hands you an equation.

CORRELATION (already known)

Module 03 measured how strongly two fields move together -- a single number, r, with no way to plug in a new x and get a guess for y.

REGRESSION (this concept)

ŷ=b0+b1x is a machine: feed it any x, it hands back a predicted y. Two numbers, b0 and b1, are the entire model.

First memory: a line is a machine that turns any x into a guess for y -- correlation only tells you the machine is worth building.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Once two things move together, the next question is "then what's my guess?"

Module 03 already showed that prior_contact_attempts and case_age_days move together with a strong Pearson r. Knowing they're related is not the same as having a way to answer "a case comes in with 4 prior contact attempts -- how many days should I expect it to take?" A line is the simplest possible answer to that question: pick two numbers, b0 and b1, and every x has a predicted y.

Pick a line, predict, subtract, square, add

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"The data looks linear, so my eyeballed line must be the best line."

False -- the two-point method only guarantees the line passes through the two rows you picked. Section 15 shows a strategy that does better on the same six points, without revealing what it is yet.

"A line's residuals should always sum to zero."

False for an arbitrary line -- this lesson's own candidate line sums to +1, not 0. That property belongs to a very specific line, the one Concept 02 derives.

"Correlation and regression are two names for the same check."

False -- r is a single number describing strength and direction; a fitted line is a working equation you can hand a new x.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

State the model form from memory

ŷ=b0+b1x -- an intercept and a slope, applied to any x to produce a predicted y.

Compute a residual and SSE for a given line and points

residual=y-ŷ for each point; SSE=Σresidual² across all points -- exactly the table in Section 04.

Explain why a lower SSE means a better fit, without any formula for finding the best line

SSE adds up how far off every prediction is, squared. Smaller total error is a better fit by definition -- HOW to find the line with the smallest possible SSE is Concept 02, not this one.

04
INTUITION

A line is a machine that turns any x into a guess for y

Feed the machine x=4 prior contact attempts, it hands back ŷ=2+3×4=14 days. Feed it x=1, it hands back ŷ=5. The machine doesn't know or care whether 14 is a good guess -- that's a separate question, answered by comparing every guess to what actually happened and adding up how wrong it was.

Analogy boundaryThe machine picture explains what a fitted line DOES. It doesn't capture how you'd pick the BEST possible machine out of every line you could draw -- Sections 09 and 15 stay deliberately silent on that, and Concept 02 answers it.

06
MECHANISM

Two-point line, then predict-subtract-square-add

STEP 1 -- FIND A CANDIDATE LINE

Pick two rows, solve for the slope and intercept that pass through both exactly. This is a shortcut -- it uses only 2 of the 6 rows and ignores the rest when choosing (b0, b1).

STEP 2 -- SCORE IT

For every row (including the two used to build the line): predict ŷ, subtract to get the residual, square it, sum all six squares into SSE.

09
MATHEMATICS

The model form and the score, nothing more

ŷ = b0 + b1·x — the model form
SSE(b0,b1) = Σ (y − ŷ)² — the score, for any candidate (b0, b1)

That's the entire mathematics this concept uses. SSE is written here as a function of (b0, b1) on purpose -- different candidate lines produce different SSE values, and a smaller SSE means a better fit. Finding the (b0, b1) that makes SSE as small as possible is a real, well-posed problem. This concept does not solve it -- it only shows what "solve it" would even mean, by computing SSE for one specific candidate. Concept 02 does the minimizing.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One candidate line, scored against the mean-only floor

Step 1 -- the two-point candidate line. Using rows 1 (x=1,y=5) and 6 (x=6,y=20): slope b1=(20-5)/(6-1)=15/5=3; intercept b0=5-3×1=2. Candidate line: ŷ=2+3x.

Step 2 -- predictions, residuals, SSE. The full table (identical to Section 04's beginner version, repeated here for the advanced worked example):

Sum of residuals = 0+1-1+3-2+0 = +1 (deliberately not zero -- an observed fact about this specific line, not a general property of every line). SSE = 0+1+1+9+4+0 = 15.

Step 3 -- the mean-only baseline (Module 01 Concept 07's own floor), recomputed fresh on these six rows. mean(y) = (5+9+10+17+15+20)/6 = 76/6 = 12.666666666666666. Baseline residuals: -7.666667, -3.666667, -2.666667, +4.333333, +2.333333, +7.333333. Baseline SSE = 157.33333333333334 (exact fraction 1416/9).

Step 4 -- the comparison. Reduction = 157.333 - 15 = 142.333. Ratio = 157.333/15 ≈ 10.489x lower SSE than the mean-only baseline. The candidate line is clearly better than guessing the average every time -- but "clearly better than the floor" is not the same claim as "the best possible line," which Section 15 addresses without answering.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Rows 1 and 6 -- residual exactly 0

The two-point method guarantees a perfect fit at the two rows used to build the line -- unsurprising, since the line was chosen to pass through them.

BOUNDARY CASE

Row 4 -- the largest single miss

x=4, y=17, ŷ=14, residual=+3, residual²=9 -- more than half of the total SSE=15 comes from this one row alone.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Extrapolating to x=20 prior contact attempts

The observed data only spans x=1 to x=6. Plugging in x=20 gives ŷ=62, a number with no support anywhere in the data this line was built from.

12
CODE

Predictions, residuals and SSE, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
rows = [(1, 5), (2, 9), (3, 10), (4, 17), (5, 15), (6, 20)]

def predict(b0, b1, x):
    return b0 + b1 * x

def sse(b0, b1, rows):
    total = 0.0
    for x, y in rows:
        resid = y - predict(b0, b1, x)
        total += resid ** 2
    return total

b0, b1 = 2, 3
residuals = [y - predict(b0, b1, x) for x, y in rows]
assert residuals == [0, 1, -1, 3, -2, 0]
assert sum(residuals) == 1          # does NOT cancel to zero
assert sse(b0, b1, rows) == 15

mean_y = sum(y for _, y in rows) / len(rows)
assert mean_y == 12.666666666666666
baseline_sse = sum((y - mean_y) ** 2 for _, y in rows)
assert round(baseline_sse, 8) == round(157.33333333333334, 8)

reduction = baseline_sse - sse(b0, b1, rows)
ratio = baseline_sse / sse(b0, b1, rows)
assert round(reduction, 4) == 142.3333
assert round(ratio, 4) == 10.4889

DIAGNOSIS

Where a fitted line breaks

EXTRAPOLATION

Predicting outside x=1 to x=6

ŷ=2+3×20=62 has no support anywhere in the training data -- a line's validity is bounded by the x-range it was fit on.

NON-LINEAR DATA (preview)

SSE stays high no matter the (b0,b1)

If the true relationship curves, no straight line -- not even the optimal one -- drives SSE anywhere near zero. Concept 11 gives the formal assumption this violates.

REALITY

Production implications

  • A fitted line is only valid inside the x-range it was trained on -- deploying it on inputs far outside that range is the extrapolation failure from Section 16, not a modeling improvement.
  • Refitting cadence and staleness are named forward to Module 17's drift material, without reopening Module 05 Concept 08's own threshold-staleness discussion -- that concept covered a classification threshold going stale, a different mechanism from a regression line going stale.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

What are the two numbers that fully define a linear regression model, and what does each control?
b0, the intercept -- the predicted y when x=0 -- and b1, the slope -- how much ŷ changes per one-unit change in x. Together, ŷ=b0+b1x.
Given ŷ=2+3x, compute the residual at x=5, y=15.
ŷ=2+3×5=17. residual=y-ŷ=15-17=-2.
Why does SSE=15 for this concept's candidate line not prove it's the best possible line on these six points?
SSE=15 only shows this line beats the mean-only baseline (157.33). It says nothing about whether a different (b0,b1) could score even lower -- that comparison is exactly what Concept 02's OLS derivation makes possible.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
(a) ŷ=2+3×7=23. (b) residual=y-ŷ=22-23=-1. (c) residual²=1. (d) Updated SSE = original 15 + 1 = 16, across all seven rows now.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why square the residuals instead of just adding up the absolute errors?
Squaring makes every term non-negative without an absolute-value discontinuity, and it penalizes large misses more than small ones. The deeper reason ordinary least squares specifically uses squares -- not just any way to make errors positive -- is named forward to Concept 02, not derived here.
Mathematical: given a line and a set of points, compute a residual and the SSE.
For each point, residual=y-ŷ where ŷ=b0+b1x; SSE is the sum of every residual squared. Section 04's table and Section 20's exercise are both worked instances of exactly this computation.
Coding: implement predict() and sse() from scratch.
Section 12 gives the full implementation -- a predict function taking (b0, b1, x) and an sse function that sums squared residuals over a list of (x, y) rows, with asserts reproducing this concept's own SSE=15 and baseline SSE≈157.33.
Debugging: a line shows suspiciously low SSE on the six rows it was fit on. What should you check?
Whether that low SSE holds on rows the line has never seen -- a line can fit its own training points closely (in the extreme, a line built from only 2 of them fits those 2 perfectly) without generalizing. This is an overfitting preview, treated lightly here and in full detail in later modules.
System design: when would you reach for linear regression over a classification model at all?
When the actual business question has a numeric answer -- "how many days will this take" rather than "will this case get relief." CFPB's Model 1 and Model 2 are both classification because their questions are yes/no; a regression model would answer a different, equally real question about resolution-time forecasting.
