MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 02

The bowl has a bottom.
Calculus finds it exactly.

Concept 01 fit a line by a two-point shortcut, scored it (SSE=15), and noticed its residuals summed to +1, not 0 -- an open question, not yet an explanation. This concept closes it. Setting both partial derivatives of SSE(b0,b1) to zero produces two linear equations, the normal equations, that any candidate line's residuals must satisfy if the line is truly optimal. Solving them on Concept 01's exact six CFPB rows gives one specific line: b1=20/7≈2.8571, b0=8/3≈2.6667. 01 · CONCEPT FIRST

OLS doesn't find a good line. It finds THE line.

TWO-POINT METHOD (Concept 01)

Pick 2 of 6 rows, draw the line through them exactly. Fast, but no guarantee it's the best fit for all 6.

ORDINARY LEAST SQUARES (this concept)

Use all 6 rows. Set the slope of the SSE surface to zero in both directions at once. The result is provably the one line with the smallest possible SSE.

First memory: "a good line" and "the SSE-minimizing line" are different claims -- OLS is the second one, and it's the only method here that can prove it.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 01 ended with an open question. This is the answer.

Concept 01's candidate line ŷ=2+3x scored SSE=15 -- clearly better than the mean-only baseline's 157.33, but there was no way to know if some OTHER (b0,b1) would score even lower. Worse, that candidate line's six residuals summed to +1, not 0, and Concept 01 explicitly left open whether that was a fluke of this particular line or true of every line. Ordinary least squares answers both questions in one move: it names the exact (b0,b1) that minimizes SSE, and it proves that line's residuals must sum to zero.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Picture a bowl. Find where it stops sloping.

SSE(b0,b1) is not one number -- it's a different number for every possible (b0,b1) pair, and plotted as a surface it looks like a bowl. Somewhere in that bowl there's a single lowest point, where the surface is flat in every direction at once. Calculus finds that point directly: take the partial derivative of SSE with respect to b0, take it with respect to b1, and set BOTH to zero. That gives two linear equations, the normal equations:

n·b0 + b1·Σx = Σy
b0·Σx + b1·Σx² = Σxy

Plugging in this dataset's own sums (n=6, Σx=21, Σy=76, Σx²=91, Σxy=316) and solving the two equations simultaneously gives b1=20/7≈2.8571 and b0=8/3≈2.6667 -- one specific line, ŷ≈2.6667+2.8571x, not a shortcut guess.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"OLS finds a good line."

Undersells it -- OLS finds THE unique line with the smallest possible SSE for this exact objective, not merely "a" reasonable one.

"Any fitted line's residuals sum to zero."

False in general. It's a guaranteed property of the specific OLS line (from the first normal equation), and it is exactly the property Concept 01's own ad hoc line failed to have.

04
INTUITION

A bowl-shaped surface, and its one flat point

Every possible (b0,b1) pair produces some SSE value. Plotted as a surface over the (b0,b1) plane, SSE looks like a bowl: high at the edges, low in the middle, with exactly one lowest point. At that point, and nowhere else, the surface is flat in every direction -- both partial derivatives are exactly zero. Calculus finds that point directly, without trying candidate after candidate.

Analogy boundaryThe bowl picture explains WHERE the minimum is (where both partials vanish). It does not by itself prove that point is unique -- that's a consequence of the bowl's shape being a specific kind of quadratic (positive-definite), which the contour ellipses in Sections 07 and 14 make visible rather than prove formally.

06
MECHANISM

Derivative, zero, two equations, solve

STEP 1 -- DIFFERENTIATE

SSE(b0,b1)=Σ(y-b0-b1x)². Take ∂SSE/∂b0=-2Σ(y-b0-b1x) and ∂SSE/∂b1=-2Σx(y-b0-b1x).

STEP 2 -- SET BOTH TO ZERO, SOLVE

∂SSE/∂b0=0 gives n·b0+b1·Σx=Σy. ∂SSE/∂b1=0 gives b0·Σx+b1·Σx²=Σxy. Two linear equations, two unknowns -- solve simultaneously.

09
MATHEMATICS

From SSE to the closed form

SSE(b0,b1) = Σ(y − b0 − b1·x)²
Normal equations: n·b0 + b1·Σx = Σy ; b0·Σx + b1·Σx² = Σxy
b1 = (n·Σxy − Σx·Σy) / (n·Σx² − (Σx)²) ; b0 = ȳ − b1·x̄
Matrix form (equivalent): β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy

The b1/b0 formulas are just the normal equations solved symbolically once, so you never have to re-derive them for a new dataset -- only re-plug the sums. The matrix form β=(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy is named as the equivalent general statement for any number of predictors; this concept stays in the 2-unknown, hand-solvable case and does not re-derive the matrix inversion in full generality.

Solving the normal equations on Concept 01's own six rows

Step 1 -- the sums. n=6, Σx=21, Σy=76, Σxy=316, Σx²=91.

Step 2 -- the closed form. b1 = (6×316 − 21×76) / (6×91 − 21²) = (1896−1596)/(546−441) = 300/105 = 20/7 ≈ 2.857142857142857. b0 = ȳ − b1·x̄ = (76 − (20/7)×21)/6 = (76−60)/6 = 16/6 = 8/3 ≈ 2.6666666666666665.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

This concept's own worked example

b1=20/7, b0=8/3, SSE=304/21≈14.4762, residuals summing to exactly 0 -- the reference result for everything else in this lesson.

BOUNDARY CASE

Σ(x−x̄)²=0 -- all-identical x

If every row had the same x, the closed-form b1 formula divides by zero (n·Σx²−(Σx)² collapses to 0) -- OLS is undefined with no variance in the predictor, a real failure mode, not a hypothetical one.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Hand-solving this with more than 2 predictors

This concept's 2-unknown hand-solve doesn't scale -- once p>2, the practical move is matrix inversion or library code (β=(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy via a solver), named forward, not derived here.

12
CODE

Sums, closed form, and the verified numbers

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
rows = [(1, 5), (2, 9), (3, 10), (4, 17), (5, 15), (6, 20)]
n = len(rows)
sx = sum(x for x, _ in rows)
sy = sum(y for _, y in rows)
sxy = sum(x * y for x, y in rows)
sxx = sum(x * x for x, _ in rows)

b1 = (n * sxy - sx * sy) / (n * sxx - sx ** 2)
b0 = (sy - b1 * sx) / n
assert round(b1, 15) == round(20 / 7, 15)
assert round(b0, 15) == round(8 / 3, 15)

def predict(x):
    return b0 + b1 * x

residuals = [y - predict(x) for x, y in rows]
sse = sum(r ** 2 for r in residuals)
assert round(sum(residuals), 9) == 0
assert round(sse, 9) == round(304 / 21, 9)

sse_candidate = 15          # Concept 01's two-point line
sse_baseline = 472 / 3      # Concept 01's mean-only baseline
ratio_vs_candidate = sse_candidate / sse
ratio_vs_baseline = sse_baseline / sse
assert round(ratio_vs_candidate, 4) == 1.0362
assert round(ratio_vs_baseline, 3) == 10.868

ECOSYSTEM

Where the matrix inversion stops being hand-rolled

scikit-learn's linear_model module -- LinearRegression().fit() -- returns the identical b0=8/3, b1=20/7 on these same six rows, but not by literally inverting XᵀX. It solves via a numerically stable SVD/lstsq decomposition instead, because direct matrix inversion becomes unstable once there are many or correlated predictors. That production reason -- never hand-invert XᵀX at scale -- is exactly what motivates Concept 03's regularization, named forward only.

DIAGNOSIS

Where the closed form breaks

ZERO PREDICTOR VARIANCE

Σ(x−x̄)²=0 -- division by zero

If every x is identical, n·Σx²−(Σx)² collapses to zero and the closed-form b1 formula is undefined -- OLS has nothing to solve for.

NEAR-COLLINEAR PREDICTORS (preview)

XᵀX becomes near-singular

With multiple, highly correlated predictors, direct matrix inversion turns numerically unstable long before it's technically undefined -- Concept 03's regularization motivation, named forward only.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: derive the normal equations from scratch.
Start from SSE(b0,b1)=Σ(y-b0-b1x)². Differentiate with respect to b0: ∂SSE/∂b0=-2Σ(y-b0-b1x); set to zero and simplify to n·b0+b1·Σx=Σy. Differentiate with respect to b1: ∂SSE/∂b1=-2Σx(y-b0-b1x); set to zero and simplify to b0·Σx+b1·Σx²=Σxy.
Conceptual: why does squared loss (not absolute loss) yield a closed form?
Squared loss is differentiable everywhere, so setting its derivative to zero produces clean linear equations to solve. Absolute loss is not differentiable at zero and its minimizer is the median, not the mean -- named here as an aside, not derived.
Mathematical: when does XᵀX become singular or near-singular, and why does that matter?
When predictors are perfectly or near-perfectly collinear (or, in the 1-predictor case, when Σ(x-x̄)²=0 -- no variance in x). XᵀX loses full rank, so (XᵀX)⁻¹ is undefined or numerically unstable, which is exactly what motivates Concept 03's regularization, named forward only.
Coding: implement the closed-form b0/b1 solve from scratch.
Section 12 gives the full implementation -- compute the five sums (n, Σx, Σy, Σxy, Σx²), plug into the b1/b0 formulas, then predictions/residuals/SSE, with asserts reproducing this concept's own b1=20/7, b0=8/3, SSE≈14.4762.
System design: OLS and MLE under Gaussian noise.
Under the assumption that residuals are Gaussian-distributed noise, minimizing SSE is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the observed data -- named here as a fact for later, formally treated in Concept 11's assumption set, not derived in this concept.
