MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 05

z already had a name.
It's the log-odds.

Concept 04 built z=w0+w1x1+w2x2 and squashed it through sigma(z) to read the result as a probability -- but it never said what z itself meant. This concept answers that: z is the log-odds of the outcome, and the identity is exact, not approximate. Starting from odds=p/(1-p), ln(odds) works out to be z itself, every time, for every row. That single fact turns the fitted weights (w1=1.5, w2=-0.5) into something a stakeholder can actually use: a one-unit increase in prior_contact_attempts multiplies the odds of relief by exp(1.5)=4.4817, and a one-unit increase in satisfaction_score multiplies it by exp(-0.5)=0.6065 -- constant multipliers on odds, but never on probability itself.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Sigmoid turns z into a probability. Log-odds is what z already was.

SIGMOID (Concept 04)

sigma(z) turns z into a probability -- a mechanism, taking z as raw input, giving p as output.

LOG-ODDS (this concept)

z itself already has a name and a meaning before any squashing happens: it's the log-odds. log-odds = ln(odds) = z, exactly, always.

First memory: log-odds = ln(odds) = z. Not close to z, not usually z -- exactly z, for every row, every time.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Raw coefficients mean nothing on the probability scale

w1=1.5 and w2=-0.5 are Concept 04's own fitted weights. On the probability scale they mean almost nothing on their own -- probability is bounded and saturating, so "add 1.5" doesn't translate into any fixed change in p. A CFPB analyst reading this model's output needs an answer to "what does 1.5 actually mean" that doesn't depend on where you started. The odds scale gives exactly that: on odds, a weight's effect is a constant multiplier, exp(w_j), no matter where you started. That's the whole reason this concept exists -- not a new model, just the scale where the existing weights finally say something stable.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Three scales, one quantity

z (linear, unbounded) becomes odds (positive, unbounded) via exponentiation, and odds becomes p (bounded 0-1) via odds/(1+odds). Reading that chain backwards is exactly what this concept does: given p, odds=p/(1-p), and log-odds=ln(odds), which turns out to just be z again.

odds = p / (1 - p)
log-odds = ln(odds) = z (proven exactly in Section 09, not approximately)

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Three scales of one number, and what "ratio" means on each

PairingThe real difference
Probability vs. odds vs. log-oddsThree monotonic transforms of the same quantity -- p in (0,1), odds in (0,infinity), log-odds in (-infinity,infinity). Monotonic means order is preserved, but the three scales are NOT arithmetically interchangeable: a feature's effect is additive on log-odds, multiplicative on odds, and neither on probability.
Odds ratio vs. "percent change in probability"exp(w_j) is a constant multiplier on odds. It is not a percent change in probability -- that number depends on where you started on the sigmoid curve, demonstrated with real numbers in b07.
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"exp(w_j) tells you the percent change in probability."

False. Going from Row C to Row F (Section 10), odds multiply by exactly 4.4817 -- but probability only goes from 0.2689 to 0.6225, a probability ratio of 2.3145, not 4.4817.

"A positive coefficient means probability goes up by that amount."

False. w1=1.5 is a log-odds unit, not a probability unit. Adding 1.5 to z does not add 1.5 to p -- p is bounded to (0,1) and can't move by an arbitrary additive amount.

"Log-odds and probability are basically interchangeable."

Only as an order-preserving relationship. Arithmetically they behave differently: log-odds add across features cleanly; probability does not.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

State odds=p/(1-p) and log-odds=ln(odds)=z from memory

Odds and log-odds are computed FROM p, but log-odds always turns out to equal the same z the model already produced.

Compute the odds ratio for a given w_j

The odds ratio for feature j is exp(w_j) -- for w1=1.5 that's exp(1.5)=4.4817; for w2=-0.5 that's exp(-0.5)=0.6065.

Explain in one sentence why exp(w_j) is not a probability-scale multiplier

Because exp(w_j) is derived from the odds ratio identity, which holds exactly on odds only -- probability's relationship to z is nonlinear (the sigmoid), so a constant odds multiplier produces a probability change that depends on the starting point.

04
INTUITION

Betting odds, and a scale that adds up cleanly

2:1 odds and 66.7% probability describe the exact same likelihood -- odds is just probability expressed as a ratio of "for" to "against" instead of a fraction of the whole. Log-odds takes that one step further: it's the scale where each feature's contribution stacks up by simple addition (z=w0+w1x1+w2x2 already is a sum) before getting exponentiated back into odds and then squashed into probability.

Analogy boundaryThe betting-odds picture explains WHAT the odds and log-odds scales mean. It doesn't explain WHY the sigmoid's specific functional form was chosen -- that stays Concept 04's own territory.

09
MATHEMATICS

The two proofs: log-odds=z, and the odds-ratio identity

p = sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^-z)
1 - p = e^-z / (1+e^-z)
odds = p/(1-p) = [1/(1+e^-z)] / [e^-z/(1+e^-z)] = 1/e^-z = e^z
log-odds = ln(odds) = ln(e^z) = z (exact, for every p, every row)

The odds-ratio identity follows directly from exponent rules. If x_j increases by 1 and every other feature stays fixed, z_new = z_old + w_j (since w_j·(x_j+1) = w_j·x_j + w_j, and every other term is unchanged). So:

odds_new = e^(z_old + w_j) = e^z_old · e^w_j = odds_old · exp(w_j)

The odds ratio, odds_new/odds_old, is exactly exp(w_j) -- a constant, regardless of what z_old was. That's the entire proof, and it's why the odds ratio is constant while the probability change never is.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean case, a boundary case, and a mistake

CLEAN CASE

Row D -- odds land exactly on e

z=1.0 gives odds=e^1.0=2.718282 exactly, and log-odds=1.000000, matching z to the decimal.

BOUNDARY CASE

z exactly 0 -- odds exactly 1

At z=0, odds=e^0=1 exactly -- the "50:50" point, where "for" and "against" are equally likely. Not framed as a decision boundary -- that term is reserved for Concept 06.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Treating exp(w_j) as a probability-scale multiplier

Grounded in Row C→F: odds went x4.4817, probability only went x2.3145. The two numbers are both real and both correct -- they just answer different questions.

CODE

Odds, log-odds, and the odds-ratio identity, asserted

PYTHON · EXTENDS CONCEPT 04'S OWN sigmoid() 
import math

def sigmoid(z):
    if z >= 0:
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-z))
    ez = math.exp(z)
    return ez / (1.0 + ez)

def odds(p):
    return p / (1.0 - p)

def log_odds(p):
    return math.log(odds(p))

W0, W1, W2 = -4.0, 1.5, -0.5

def z_of(x1, x2):
    return W0 + W1 * x1 + W2 * x2

rows = [
    ("A", 1, 4), ("B", 2, 3), ("C", 3, 3), ("D", 4, 2), ("E", 6, 1),
]

for label, x1, x2 in rows:
    z = z_of(x1, x2)
    p = sigmoid(z)
    assert abs(log_odds(p) - z) < 1e-9  # the core identity, for every row

# Row F, plus the odds-ratio identity for both weights.
odds_C = odds(sigmoid(z_of(3, 3)))
odds_F = odds(sigmoid(z_of(4, 3)))
odds_D = odds(sigmoid(z_of(4, 2)))

assert abs(odds_F / odds_C - math.exp(W1)) < 1e-9  # x1's odds ratio == exp(w1)
assert abs(odds_F / odds_D - math.exp(W2)) < 1e-9  # x2's odds ratio == exp(w2)

DIAGNOSIS

Where odds-ratio reasoning goes wrong

OVER-INTERPRETING ODDS RATIOS

Reading them as probability-scale changes

The single most common real-world miscommunication -- grounded in Row C→F: 4.4817x odds is not 4.4817x probability. The correction is Section 10's own contrast, restated as needed.

MULTICOLLINEARITY

Distorting an individual coefficient's read

When features are correlated, an individual w_j's odds ratio can shift and become unstable -- Module 04 Concept 06's VIF, cited, not retaught.

UNREGULARIZED, UNSTABLE COEFFICIENTS

Before shrinkage

Concept 03's ridge/lasso shrinkage pulls w_j toward 0 -- and therefore pulls its odds ratio toward 1, i.e. toward "no effect," named back to that concept directly.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
Odds ratio equals probability ratioNo -- Row C to Row F: odds ratio 4.4817, probability ratio only 2.3145. Both real, both correct, different questions.

Production implications

  • Report odds ratios accurately in model documentation -- especially in a regulated context like CFPB, where a coefficient misquoted as a probability effect in a compliance narrative is a real, avoidable error.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why are coefficients easier to interpret in log-odds/odds terms than in raw probability?
Because a feature's effect is additive on log-odds and multiplicative on odds -- both constant, regardless of where you started. On probability, the same coefficient produces a different-sized change depending on the starting probability, since the sigmoid saturates near 0 and 1.
Mathematical: derive the odds-ratio identity from z_new=z_old+w_j.
odds_new=e^z_new=e^(z_old+w_j)=e^z_old·e^w_j=odds_old·exp(w_j), by exponent rules. Dividing both sides by odds_old gives odds_new/odds_old=exp(w_j) exactly.
Coding: implement odds() and log_odds(), and assert the core identity.
Section 12 gives the full implementation -- odds(p)=p/(1-p), log_odds(p)=ln(odds(p)), with an assertion that log_odds(sigmoid(z)) equals z to within float tolerance for every row.
System design / production: how do you report a coefficient's meaning to a non-technical stakeholder without overclaiming a probability effect?
Lead with the odds-ratio sentence ("multiplies the odds by about 4.48x, other things equal"), log both the raw coefficient and its exp() in the model card, and explicitly flag that the probability-scale effect is not constant and depends on the starting point -- exactly the Row C→F contrast from Section 10.
