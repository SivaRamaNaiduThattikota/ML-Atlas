MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 07

This boundary wasn't fit. It was derived from two bell curves.
And it curves, the moment the variances differ.

Concepts 04-06 all built the exact same boundary from a fitted weight vector, then reread it three ways -- as a probability, as log-odds, as a line. This concept starts over completely: no weights, no loss function. Given a new feature, resolution_days, and each class's own Gaussian mean and variance, Bayes' rule alone tells you where the predicted class flips. Completely different route.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

A boundary derived from two bell curves, not a fitted line

Every boundary so far in this module came from minimizing a loss over a weight vector. This concept asks a different question entirely: if you already know (or can estimate) how each class's own feature values are distributed, can you build a boundary directly from that, with no fitting step at all? The answer is yes -- and the shape of the answer depends entirely on one assumption: do the two classes share a variance, or not?

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Two routes to the same kind of answer, starting from different places

FITTED WEIGHTS (Concepts 04-06)

Start with labeled rows, minimize cross-entropy loss over a weight vector, end up with z=w0+w1x1+w2x2. The boundary is wherever that fitted z equals 0.

CLASS-CONDITIONAL GAUSSIANS (this concept)

Start with each class's own mean and variance for one feature, use Bayes' rule to compare which class's density is higher at any given x. The boundary is wherever those two densities cross.

First memory: same JOB (draw a boundary), completely different STARTING POINT. Concepts 04-06 started from labeled data and a loss function. This concept starts from an assumed distribution per class -- no loss function anywhere in the mechanism.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Sometimes you know the distributions before you have anything to fit against

Logistic regression needs labeled rows to minimize a loss. But sometimes what you actually know is different: "relief cases tend to take about 6 days to resolve, give or take 2; no-relief cases tend to take about 2 days, give or take 1." That's not a labeled dataset -- it's a distributional fact, and it's enough on its own to build a boundary, via Bayes' rule. That's the entire reason linear and quadratic discriminant analysis exist: a second, genuinely different route to the same kind of decision rule.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

A new feature, resolution_days, and two Gaussians

resolution_days: the number of days between a CFPB complaint's submission and the agency's decision. Never used anywhere earlier in this module. Two classes, assumed Gaussian, equal priors (each class equally likely before seeing resolution_days -- this lesson's stated simplifying assumption):

ClassMeaningMean (mu)Std dev (sigma)
0No relief -- resolved quickly2 days1 day
1Relief -- extended review6 days2 days

Relief cases take longer on average AND are more variable -- some resolve fast, some drag on for complex reasons. That variance gap is exactly the condition that makes LDA's shared-variance assumption wrong here, and it's the whole reason this concept needs both LDA and QDA, not just one of them.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Bayes' rule, two ways to resolve the "which variance" question

Bayes' rule (scikit-learn, LDA/QDA): P(y=k|x) is proportional to P(x|y=k)P(y=k), so with equal priors, the predicted class is just whichever class-conditional density P(x|y=k) is higher at x. Force both classes to share one variance and the boundary is a single point, the midpoint of the two means: x*=(2+6)/2=4.0. Let each class keep its own variance and the boundary is a genuine quadratic, with two roots: -2.327 and 3.660.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

LDA vs. logistic regression, and LDA vs. QDA

PairingThe real difference
LDA vs. logistic regressionBoth produce a LINEAR boundary -- same shape. But they get there by completely different routes (Bayes' rule over Gaussians vs. minimizing cross-entropy over weights), and there's no guarantee the two numeric lines coincide on real data.
LDA vs. QDASame derivation family, same Bayes'-rule mechanism. The only difference is one assumption: shared covariance (LDA, linear boundary) versus per-class covariance (QDA, quadratic boundary).
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"LDA and logistic regression must give the same line."

False. Both are linear in shape, but one is derived from Gaussian densities and one from a fitted loss -- they can, and often do, land on different numeric lines even on the same data.

"QDA is always better, since it's more flexible."

False. QDA needs a full, separate variance (in general, covariance) estimate per class, which costs more data to estimate reliably than LDA's one shared estimate -- a full assumptions/violations discussion is Concept 11's own territory, named forward only here.

06
MECHANISM

Two steps, traced once for LDA and once for QDA

STEP 1 -- WRITE THE LOG-POSTERIOR FOR EACH CLASS

Under equal priors, comparing posteriors reduces to comparing log-densities: -ln(sigma_k) - (x-mu_k)^2/(2*sigma_k^2), one term per class.

STEP 2 -- SET THEM EQUAL AND SOLVE FOR x

LDA (sigma0=sigma1=sigma): the -ln(sigma) terms and the 1/(2*sigma^2) factors cancel completely, leaving a linear equation whose only solution is x*=(mu0+mu1)/2=4.0. QDA (sigma0 != sigma1): the sigma terms survive, leaving a genuine quadratic, A*x^2+B*x+C=0, with roots -2.327 and 3.660.

09
MATHEMATICS

From Bayes' rule to two boundaries

The univariate Gaussian density: f(x) = (1/(sigma*sqrt(2*pi))) * exp(-(x-mu)^2/(2*sigma^2)). With equal priors, the predicted class at x is whichever class's f(x) is larger. Setting f0(x)=f1(x) and taking logs of both sides:

-ln(sigma0) - (x-mu0)^2/(2*sigma0^2) = -ln(sigma1) - (x-mu1)^2/(2*sigma1^2)

LDA case (sigma0=sigma1=sigma): the -ln(sigma) terms cancel outright, and the 1/(2*sigma^2) factor cancels from both squared terms, leaving (x-mu0)^2=(x-mu1)^2. Expanding and simplifying gives a single linear solution:

x* = (mu0 + mu1) / 2 = (2 + 6) / 2 = 4.0

This holds for ANY shared sigma -- the boundary is sigma-invariant whenever both classes use the same one, which is exactly why Section 14's lab shows the LDA line staying fixed no matter how far the sigma sliders move, as long as they stay equal to each other.

QDA case (sigma0 != sigma1): multiplying out the equality above and collecting every term in x gives a general quadratic, A*x^2+B*x+C=0, where:

A = sigma0^2 - sigma1^2
B = 2*(sigma1^2*mu0 - sigma0^2*mu1)
C = sigma0^2*mu1^2 - sigma1^2*mu0^2 - 2*sigma0^2*sigma1^2*ln(sigma0/sigma1)

Plugging in mu0=2, sigma0=1, mu1=6, sigma1=2: A=1-4=-3, B=2*(4*2-1*6)=4, C=1*36-4*4-2*1*4*ln(0.5)=20+8*ln(2)=25.545177444479562. The quadratic formula on -3x^2+4x+25.545177444479562=0 gives a discriminant of B^2-4AC=16+306.542129...=322.542129..., and two real roots:

x = -2.3265763225683034 and x = 3.6599096559016364

When A=0 exactly (equal variances), this same formula degenerates to the single linear root x=-C/B, which reduces algebraically to (mu0+mu1)/2 -- LDA is the A=0 special case of this one general formula, not a separate mechanism. Both roots above were independently confirmed by plugging back into the two Gaussian densities directly (the differences at each root are at float-precision noise level, roughly 1e-17 to 1e-20) and by a wide brute-force scan of f0(x)-f1(x) that found sign changes at exactly these two points and nowhere else.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Region behavior, and the disagreement at x=3.8

Class 0 (the tighter distribution) wins the middle band, roughly (-2.327, 3.660) -- the region containing its own mean, 2. Class 1 (the wider distribution) wins BOTH outer tails: everything below -2.327 AND everything above 3.660. Its larger variance makes its density decay more slowly, so it eventually overtakes class 0's density even far past class 0's own mean on the far side.

3.80 (no relief)1 (relief)DISAGREE

At x=3.8: f0(3.8)=0.078950, f1(3.8)=0.108926. Since f1 is larger, QDA predicts class 1 (relief); LDA, using only the sigma-blind midpoint 4.0, still predicts class 0 (no relief) because 3.8<4.0. The two models genuinely disagree on this exact point, using the exact same mu0=2 and mu1=6 -- the disagreement comes entirely from whether the variance gap is respected (QDA) or ignored (LDA).

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean case, a boundary case, and a genuine QDA caveat

CLEAN CASE

x=1 -- both models agree

1 is inside the middle band (-2.327, 3.660) and below LDA's 4.0 midpoint -- both LDA and QDA predict class 0 (no relief).

BOUNDARY CASE

x=4.0 exactly -- both models still agree

LDA's own rule (x>=4.0 predicts class 1) and QDA's density comparison both land on class 1 at exactly this point, despite the two models using entirely different formulas to get there.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

x=-5 -- assuming "far from mu1" means "safely class 0"

-5 is far below class 1's mean of 6, but it's also below QDA's lower boundary of -2.327, which sits in class 1's tail territory. QDA actually assigns x=-5 to class 1, not class 0 -- an unintuitive but real consequence of class 1's larger variance, and a genuine caveat about how far a Gaussian assumption can be trusted at extreme values (Section 16).

12
CODE

The general closed form, implemented directly

PYTHON · GAUSSIAN PDF, LDA MIDPOINT, QDA ROOTS 
import math

MU0, SIGMA0 = 2.0, 1.0
MU1, SIGMA1 = 6.0, 2.0

def pdf(x, mu, sigma):
    coeff = 1.0 / (sigma * math.sqrt(2 * math.pi))
    return coeff * math.exp(-((x - mu) ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2))

def lda_boundary(mu0, mu1):
    """The exact midpoint -- true for any SHARED sigma, since sigma
    cancels completely out of the equality f0(x)=f1(x)."""
    return (mu0 + mu1) / 2

def qda_boundary_roots(mu0, sigma0, mu1, sigma1):
    """General A/B/C closed form. Degenerates to the LDA midpoint
    when sigma0 == sigma1 (A == 0)."""
    s0sq, s1sq = sigma0 ** 2, sigma1 ** 2
    a = s0sq - s1sq
    b = 2 * (s1sq * mu0 - s0sq * mu1)
    c = s0sq * mu1 ** 2 - s1sq * mu0 ** 2 - 2 * s0sq * s1sq * math.log(sigma0 / sigma1)
    if abs(a) < 1e-9:
        return [-c / b]
    disc = b ** 2 - 4 * a * c
    if disc < 0:
        return []
    root1 = (-b + math.sqrt(disc)) / (2 * a)
    root2 = (-b - math.sqrt(disc)) / (2 * a)
    return sorted([root1, root2])

assert abs(lda_boundary(MU0, MU1) - 4.0) < 1e-9

roots = qda_boundary_roots(MU0, SIGMA0, MU1, SIGMA1)
assert abs(roots[0] - (-2.3265763225683034)) < 1e-6
assert abs(roots[1] - 3.6599096559016364) < 1e-6

# The worked example's disagreement point.
x_test = 3.8
lda_predicts = 1 if x_test >= lda_boundary(MU0, MU1) else 0
qda_predicts = 1 if pdf(x_test, MU1, SIGMA1) > pdf(x_test, MU0, SIGMA0) else 0
assert lda_predicts == 0 and qda_predicts == 1

DIAGNOSIS

Where each method actually fails

QDA'S TAIL ROOT ISN'T ALWAYS PHYSICALLY PLAUSIBLE

x=-2.327 is a negative "day" count

resolution_days can never actually be negative, so QDA's lower boundary is partly an artifact of assuming an unbounded Gaussian past zero. Honestly reporting that limitation is more useful than hiding a cleaner-looking but incomplete example.

LDA IS BLIND TO A REAL VARIANCE DIFFERENCE

The solid line in Section 14's lab never moves for sigma alone

Dragging either sigma slider away from the other visibly moves the QDA boundaries below, while LDA's own line stays frozen at (mu0+mu1)/2 -- a live demonstration that LDA simply cannot see a variance gap, by construction.

Incorrect beliefCorrection

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Means and variances get estimated from real, sometimes small per-class samples -- a class with few examples gives a noisier variance estimate, and QDA's quadratic term is more sensitive to that noise than LDA's single shared estimate is.
  • Report an assumption's limits alongside its results -- QDA's own lower boundary here (a negative day count) is a real artifact of the Gaussian assumption, not a hidden detail.
  • Choosing LDA versus QDA in a real pipeline is itself a modeling decision worth documenting, not a default -- Concept 11 covers the fuller assumptions/violations discussion, named forward only.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why does LDA's boundary reduce to a line even in high dimensions?
Because the shared-covariance assumption makes the quadratic terms in the log-posterior difference cancel identically across all classes, leaving only linear terms in x -- the same cancellation this lesson's 1D case shows algebraically, just with a covariance matrix in place of a single sigma.
Mathematical: derive the LDA midpoint from the log-posterior equality.
Set -ln(sigma)-(x-mu0)^2/(2*sigma^2) equal to -ln(sigma)-(x-mu1)^2/(2*sigma^2) (same shared sigma on both sides). The -ln(sigma) terms cancel, then multiplying through by -2*sigma^2 gives (x-mu0)^2=(x-mu1)^2. Expanding both sides and canceling the x^2 terms leaves a linear equation whose unique solution is x*=(mu0+mu1)/2.
Coding: implement qda_boundary_roots.
Section 12's qda_boundary_roots(mu0, sigma0, mu1, sigma1) computes A, B, C from the general closed form, falls back to the single linear root -C/B when abs(A) is near zero, and otherwise applies the quadratic formula to return both real roots sorted ascending.
System design: when would you choose LDA or QDA over logistic regression in a real pipeline?
When you have strong reason to believe (or can estimate) each class's feature distribution directly -- for example from domain knowledge or a small labeled sample -- rather than enough labeled data to reliably fit a discriminative loss. LDA in particular can be more stable than logistic regression when classes are well-separated and roughly Gaussian; QDA trades that stability for flexibility, at the cost of needing enough data per class to estimate a separate covariance reliably.
