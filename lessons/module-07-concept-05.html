01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Same name, two shapes so far

Concept 03 scored three binary flags -- attachment, repeat, channel. Concept 04 scored word counts over a vocabulary. Both were called "Naive Bayes." Question this concept answers: are these the same algorithm, or different flavors wearing one name?

First memory: They're the same skeleton -- Bayes' rule plus conditional independence -- wearing three different likelihood models, matched to three different feature types.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Naming the taxonomy

scikit-learn ships three real, distinct classes for this exact reason: GaussianNB, MultinomialNB, and BernoulliNB (scikit-learn, Naive Bayes). Every one of them keeps the same product-rule skeleton and the same conditional-independence assumption from Concept 03 -- the only thing that changes across variants is the shape of P(feature|class), matched to whether a feature is continuous, a count, or a yes/no flag.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Concept 03, retconned

Concept 03's three binary flags were Bernoulli-style features all along, even though that concept never used the word. Each flag is a per-class coin flip: P(flag=1|class)=p, P(flag=0|class)=1-p. Concept 03 never named this formally -- this concept gives it its name in hindsight, confirmed by scikit-learn's real BernoulliNB class, built for "multivariate Bernoulli models."

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Concept 04, cited -- not rebuilt

Concept 04's word-count model is exactly MultinomialNB (scikit-learn, MultinomialNB). Its posteriors are cited back verbatim, not rederived: on the test document "refund error thanks," Concept 04 reached P(escalate|x)=0.615385 (8/13) versus P(resolve|x)=0.384615 (5/13). No Laplace smoothing walkthrough repeats here -- it's Concept 04's job, done.

Same product rule as Concepts 03 and 04 -- these two just finally have their formal scikit-learn names.
05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

The new one: GaussianNB

Neither flags nor counts fit continuous features like a dollar amount, days-to-response, or a credit score. GaussianNB handles exactly this: instead of a count ratio or a presence probability, each class gets its own bell curve (a mean and a spread) per feature, estimated straight from that class's training data (scikit-learn, GaussianNB). The likelihood is read off that curve's height at the observed value.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Short Gaussian intuition, non-worked

Picture "dispute amount in dollars" as one continuous feature. Escalate-class disputes tend to cluster around a higher average amount than resolve-class disputes. A brand-new $900 dispute sits closer to the escalate curve's peak than the resolve curve's peak -- so GaussianNB's likelihood term favors escalate for that feature, the same way a higher word count favored a class in Concept 04. No dataset is built here; the full derivation and equation live in the advanced section below.

The numbers diverge from the same six documents

TWO TALLIES, ONE DATASET"refund" is 3 total occurrences in escalate, but only 2 of 3 escalate documents contain it at all.

Multinomial counts every repeat across all of a class's text. Bernoulli counts how many documents in a class contain the word at least once -- a genuinely different number from the identical six documents.

Same test document, same training documents, same Laplace-style smoothing convention -- but different math produces a different confidence, not just a different-looking intermediate step.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

The takeaway

Both models call it escalate here

But the confidence gap between 0.615385 and 0.529412 is real, not rounding -- it comes from a genuine difference in what each model's likelihood term can see.

Bernoulli sees absence; Multinomial can't

BernoulliNB explicitly scores the two words absent from the test document (delay, please). MultinomialNB has no term for a word that never appears in x at all -- it only ever multiplies over present tokens.

Rule of thumb: match the variant to the feature type

Continuous measurements → Gaussian. Word or event counts → Multinomial. Yes/no presence flags → Bernoulli.

TAXONOMY

Three variants, one shared skeleton

Bayes' rule stays identical across all three: P(class|x) ∝ P(class) × ∏ P(x_i|class). The only moving part is the likelihood term P(x_i|class) -- its shape changes to match the feature type.

05
TECHNICAL MECHANISM

GaussianNB: a bell curve per class, per feature

For a continuous feature, GaussianNB estimates a per-class mean μ_c and variance σ_c² directly from that class's training data (scikit-learn, GaussianNB). The Gaussian probability density function substitutes for the count-ratio term (Multinomial) or the presence-probability term (Bernoulli) inside the exact same product-of-likelihoods skeleton -- only the shape of one factor changes.

06
MATHEMATICS

The Gaussian density formula

f(x) = (1 / √(2πσ_c²)) · exp(−(x − μ_c)² / 2σ_c²)
READING THE FORMULA, PER CLASS c 
mu_c    = mean of feature x within class c's training rows
sigma_c2 = variance of feature x within class c's training rows
// likelihood of an observed value x under class c:
f(x) = (1 / sqrt(2*Math.PI*sigma_c2)) * Math.exp(-((x - mu_c) ** 2) / (2 * sigma_c2))
// a taller curve at x means a higher likelihood for that class

The normal distribution's own defining property grounds this: it's fully specified by its mean and variance, and its density falls off symmetrically the further a value sits from the mean (Wikipedia, Normal distribution).

11
DIAGNOSIS

Raw products crash to zero, for two different reasons

Multinomial raw: Escalate = 0.333333 × 0.333333 × 0.000000 = 0. Resolve = 0.111111 × 0.000000 × 0.444444 = 0.
Bernoulli raw: Escalate = 0.666667 × (1−1.000000) × 0.666667 × 0.000000 × (1−0.000000) = 0. Resolve = 0.333333 × (1−0.000000) × 0.000000 × 1.000000 × (1−1.000000) = 0.

Multinomial crashes because a present test word ("thanks" for escalate, "error" for resolve) never occurred in that class's training set. Bernoulli crashes for a mirror-image reason: "delay" is present in every single escalate training document (probability 1), so its absence in x forces a zero factor -- and "please" does the same for resolve. Bernoulli's zero comes partly from an always-present word's absence; Multinomial can't even represent that failure mode, since it only ever multiplies over words that appear in x.

12
TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Laplace smoothing, side by side

Multinomial: theta_hat_yi = (count + 1) / (N + 5)
Bernoulli: theta_hat_yi = (documents-containing + 1) / (documents-in-class + 2)

Both use alpha=1, scikit-learn's default for MultinomialNB and BernoulliNB alike. The denominators differ because they're smoothing different quantities: Multinomial adds the vocabulary size (5) to a total-word-count denominator; Bernoulli adds 2 (one for each possible binary outcome) to a document-count denominator.

14
DIAGNOSIS

The point

On the identical test document and identical six training documents, MultinomialNB gives 0.615385 vs. 0.384615 while BernoulliNB gives 0.529412 vs. 0.470588 -- both name escalate as the predicted class here, but the posteriors are genuinely different numbers, not a rounding artifact. Since each present word appears exactly once in this test document, the count-based and presence-based factors for those three words are numerically similar fractions of a different denominator -- the real driver of the gap is that BernoulliNB explicitly scores the two absent vocabulary words (delay, please) as part of its likelihood, while MultinomialNB has no mechanism to use absence at all. Bernoulli asks "does this word occur, yes or no, and does its absence elsewhere say anything"; Multinomial asks "how many times did each occurring word show up."

CODE

Verification approach, reproduced conceptually

JAVASCRIPT · SAME RAW WORD LISTS, TALLIED TWO WAYS 
const docs = {
  escalate: ["refund refund delay","error delay refund","delay error error"],
  resolve: ["thanks please thanks","please thanks refund","thanks please please"],
};
function multiTally(sentences) {
  const counts = {}; let total = 0;
  sentences.forEach(s => s.split(" ").forEach(w => { counts[w] = (counts[w]||0)+1; total += 1; }));
  return { counts, total };
}
function bernTally(sentences, vocab) {
  const df = {}; vocab.forEach(w => df[w] = 0);
  sentences.forEach(s => { const set = new Set(s.split(" "));
    vocab.forEach(w => { if (set.has(w)) df[w] += 1; }); });
  return df;
}
const vocab = ["refund","delay","error","thanks","please"];
const eMulti = multiTally(docs.escalate), rMulti = multiTally(docs.resolve);
const eBern = bernTally(docs.escalate, vocab), rBern = bernTally(docs.resolve, vocab);
// eMulti.counts.refund === 3, eBern.refund === 2 -- same documents, different tally
// smoothed posteriors: multinomial {postE: 0.6153846153846153, postR: 0.38461538461538464}
//                       bernoulli   {postE: 0.5294117647058822, postR: 0.47058823529411775}

MECHANICS

How the lab computes both scores at once

For each slider word w with test count c: multinomialFactor(w,class) = P_multi(w|class)^c (c=0 gives 1, contributing nothing). bernoulliFactor(w,class) = c>0 ? P_bern(w|class) : (1 − P_bern(w|class)) -- Bernoulli only cares whether c is zero or nonzero, never how large. "delay" and "please" are fixed absent because they let Bernoulli's absence-scoring mechanism run without a slider muddying it -- they never enter the Multinomial product at all, which is why the two tables have 5 rows and 3 rows respectively. That row-count asymmetry is intentional: it's the whole demonstration that Bernoulli scores every vocabulary word while Multinomial only scores the ones present in x. The .gate-readout block shows all four posteriors at once; the .gate-verdict string names each model's predicted class and calls out whether the two models agree or disagree, and by how much.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

A feature is a continuous dollar amount. Which Naive Bayes variant, and why?
GaussianNB -- it models each class's distribution over that feature as its own normal curve (mean and variance), reading the likelihood off the curve's height at the observed value, rather than a count ratio or a presence probability.
A feature is a yes/no support-ticket flag. Which variant, and why?
BernoulliNB -- exactly Concept 03's mechanism, one per-class coin-flip probability per flag, scoring both presence and absence.
Why does BernoulliNB score words that never appear in a document, while MultinomialNB cannot?
BernoulliNB's likelihood is a product over every vocabulary feature, present or not -- absence contributes its own (1−p) factor. MultinomialNB's likelihood is a product only over word tokens that actually occur in x; it has no term for a word count of zero.
What does setting BernoulliNB's binarize parameter do to count data fed into it?
It thresholds every value above the given cutoff (default 0.0) to 1 and everything else to 0 before scoring, silently collapsing all repeat counts to a single "occurred" flag -- discarding the exact information MultinomialNB is built to use.
22
CORRECTION

Common misconception

"Bernoulli" and "Multinomial" are not interchangeable just because both handle discrete features.Feeding raw multinomial count data straight into BernoulliNB without understanding its binarize threshold silently collapses every nonzero count to 1, changing the model's behavior without any error or warning. Match the variant to what the feature actually represents -- presence/absence versus a genuine count -- not just to "discrete versus continuous."
