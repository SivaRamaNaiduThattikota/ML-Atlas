MODULE 07 · CONCEPT 07

The line doesn't bend.
The space underneath it does.

Concept 06 built one straight max-margin line by hand, on a dataset small enough to separate with a ruler. This concept hands that same margin machinery a dataset a straight line cannot touch -- two resolve points and two escalate points whose convex hulls cross at the origin -- and adds exactly one new feature, the squared distance from the center, so the same straight-line math works again in the new space. That's the kernel trick: not a new mechanism, and not a fancier line, but the observation that a curved boundary in the original features is often just a flat one in a few more.

In raw (x1,x2), the two classes' convex hulls -- the segment R1-R2 and the segment E1-E2 -- cross exactly at the origin, so no straight line separates them; confirmed by a hull-intersection test, a 1,000,000-direction angular sweep (best achievable gap: -1.0, i.e. never positive), and a Perceptron that fails to converge after 20,000 epochs. Adding one feature, z=x1²+x2² (R1, R2 -> z=1; E1, E2 -> z=16), makes them separable by the flat plane z=8.5 with margin 7.500000 -- and by symmetry, all four points become support vectors. A live scikit-learn check confirms this: a linear SVC gets 50% accuracy on the raw features and 100% on the mapped ones (fitted margin 7.4995), and an RBF-kernel SVC gets 100% directly on the raw features, without any manual mapping at all.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

One feature, and the line works again

Concept 06 stopped at hard-margin, straight lines only. This concept asks what happens when a straight line genuinely cannot separate the classes -- and shows that the fix is sometimes just one more feature, not a new algorithm.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Still the same mechanism -- given a harder dataset

Concept 06 committed to one straight line and named the points that pinned it in place. Nothing about that commitment changes here. What changes is the dataset: this one cannot be separated by ANY straight line in its original two features, no matter how the margin math is run. The kernel trick is what happens next -- not a new way to classify, but a new space to draw the same kind of line in.

First memory: The kernel trick doesn't replace margin-maximization -- it gives it a space where a straight line actually exists.
02 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

A dataset a line can't split

Resolve (y=-1): R1=(1,0), R2=(-1,0). Escalate (y=+1): E1=(0,4), E2=(0,-4). Resolve sits close to the center on the x1-axis; Escalate sits far from the center on the x2-axis. Try to draw one straight line with all of Resolve on one side and all of Escalate on the other -- every line through the middle of this cross-shaped arrangement clips at least one point on the wrong side.

03 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Proving it can't be split -- not just eyeballing it

The segment joining R1 and R2 and the segment joining E1 and E2 both pass straight through the origin -- they cross. A known result for linear separability says two finite point sets are separable by a straight line if and only if their convex hulls do not intersect (Wikipedia, Linear separability, cited from Concept 06). Since these two hulls visibly cross, no line exists -- confirmed three independent ways in the advanced section below.

04 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

The fix is one feature, not a different algorithm

Resolve points sit at distance 1 from the origin; Escalate points sit at distance 4. That radius is exactly the information a straight line in (x1, x2) can't see -- it can only compare linear combinations of x1 and x2, never a squared distance. So hand the margin math a third feature that IS that squared distance: z = x1² + x2². This particular fix works for this particular dataset -- it isn't a universal cure, and general kernels (named forward below) go much further than one hand-picked feature.

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

Lifting the four points into 3D

Apply φ(x1, x2) = (x1, x2, x1² + x2²) to every point. R1=(1,0) becomes (1,0,1). R2=(-1,0) becomes (-1,0,1). E1=(0,4) becomes (0,4,16). E2=(0,-4) becomes (0,-4,16). Resolve's new coordinate is always 1; Escalate's is always 16 -- a huge, clean gap that didn't exist in the original two features.

06 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

The answer: a flat plane at z=8.5, margin 7.5

Because Resolve's z-values (1, 1) are both smaller than Escalate's z-values (16, 16), any threshold in between separates them perfectly. The max-margin choice sits exactly halfway: z=8.5. In properly normalized form that's w=(0, 0, 0.133333), b=-1.133333, and the margin -- the distance from that plane to the nearest point of either class -- works out to 1/||w|| = 7.500000.

07 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Every point becomes a support vector

Checking y·(w·x+b) for all four lifted points gives exactly 1.000000, every time. That's not a coincidence -- it's the dataset's own symmetry: R1 and R2 are mirror images across x1, and E1 and E2 are mirror images across x2, so the max-margin plane can't favor one over the other. All four points are support vectors, unlike Concept 06's example, where two of four points were free to move.

08 · YOUR PROJECT MAPPING

This isn't a manual step in real tooling -- it's automatic

THE MAP φ IS NEVER BUILT BY HANDscikit-learn's kernel='rbf', fit directly on the raw (x1, x2) features, classifies all four points correctly.

No one computed x1²+x2² first -- the kernel function stands in for the whole feature map. Verified live: SVC(kernel='rbf', gamma=0.05).fit(X, y) reaches 100% accuracy on the untouched two-feature data.

THE MANUAL VERSION STILL WORKS TOOFeeding the same SVC the 3 hand-built features (x1, x2, x1²+x2²) with kernel='linear' also reaches 100%, margin 7.4995.

Both routes land on the same answer -- one builds the map explicitly, the other never does. That gap is the entire kernel trick.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

The takeaway

Not every dataset is linearly separable

R1/R2 versus E1/E2 have crossing convex hulls -- proven, not assumed, three independent ways.

One more feature can restore separability

Adding z=x1²+x2² turns a crossing pattern into two cleanly stacked layers, margin 7.5.

The trick is skipping the feature-building step

A kernel function computes what the mapped dot product WOULD be, directly from the original points -- scikit-learn's RBF kernel does this live, with no φ ever materialized.

Teach-back questions

  1. Why do R1 and R2's convex hull crossing E1 and E2's prove -- not just suggest -- that no line separates them?
  2. What specific piece of information does z=x1²+x2² carry that x1 and x2 alone cannot?
  3. If a kernel function never builds the feature map explicitly, what does it compute instead?

01
ORIENTATION

An extension of Concept 06's mechanism, not a fourth one

Module 07 has one mechanism per concept-family so far: neighbor-voting (01-02), Bayes-rule scoring (03-05), and margin-maximization (06). This concept does not add a fourth. It stays inside margin-maximization entirely and asks a narrower question: what happens when the two classes cannot be separated by any straight hyperplane in the features you started with? The kernel trick's answer is to change the space, not the mechanism.

02
CITATION

Reused primitives from Concept 06

Three things are cited directly from Concept 06 and not rebuilt: the margin condition y_i(w·x_i+b) ≥ 1, the definition of a support vector as a point where that condition holds with exact equality, and the hard-margin objective of minimizing ||w|| subject to that condition for every training point. Nothing about any of these changes here -- only the coordinates they're evaluated on.

03
ARCHITECTURE

The dataset, chosen to fail on purpose

Resolve (y=-1): R1=(1,0), R2=(-1,0). Escalate (y=+1): E1=(0,4), E2=(0,-4). Unlike Concept 06's diagonal 4-point set, these two classes are arranged so that Resolve straddles the origin along one axis and Escalate straddles it along the other -- a cross, not a diagonal band. This is deliberate: it is the smallest configuration where the two classes' convex hulls provably intersect.

04
MATHEMATICS

Formalizing "not linearly separable"

Two finite point sets are linearly separable if and only if their convex hulls do not intersect.

With two points per class, each class's convex hull is simply the line segment joining them. So the entire separability question reduces to one geometry check: does segment R1-R2 intersect segment E1-E2? (Wikipedia, Support vector machine states the same hull-based characterization of separability used here.)

05
WORKED EXAMPLE

Check 1 -- convex-hull (segment) intersection

Segment R1-R2 runs along the x1-axis from (-1,0) to (1,0); segment E1-E2 runs along the x2-axis from (0,-4) to (0,4). A standard segment-intersection test (orientation of endpoints, cross products) confirms they cross at exactly one point: the origin. Since the hulls intersect, the dataset is not linearly separable -- by the theorem in s04, not by inspection alone.

06
DIAGNOSIS

Check 2 -- brute-force angular sweep (2D)

Sweeping 1,000,000 candidate directions θ ∈ [0, π) and, for each, checking whether some threshold separates the two classes' projections finds a best achievable gap of -1.000000 -- negative at every single direction tested, meaning the classes' projections always overlap by at least 1 unit. No rotation of a line ever separates this dataset.

07
DIAGNOSIS

Check 3 -- the Perceptron never converges

The Perceptron algorithm provably converges to zero training error in finite steps if and only if the data is linearly separable (Novikoff's theorem). Run for 20,000 epochs on this dataset, it never converges -- its weight vector cancels back to (0,0) every full pass, a direct symptom of the dataset's own mirror symmetry working against any single separating direction. Corroborating evidence, not a standalone proof, but consistent with Checks 1 and 2.

08
ARCHITECTURE

The feature map -- and where it comes from

φ(x1, x2) = (x1, x2, x1² + x2²)

This is not an arbitrary choice invented for this lesson -- it is the exact feature map used in Wikipedia's own canonical illustration of the kernel trick: "SVM with feature map given by φ((a,b)) = (a,b,a²+b²) ... The training points are mapped to a 3-dimensional space where a separating hyperplane can be easily found" (Wikipedia, Kernel method). The associated kernel, k(x,y) = x·y + ||x||²||y||², is quoted directly from the same source and re-derived independently for this dataset in s14-s15.

09
TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Deriving the lifted-space hyperplane by hand

Applying φ: R1→(1,0,1), R2→(-1,0,1), E1→(0,4,16), E2→(0,-4,16). Resolve's z-coordinate is always 1; Escalate's is always 16. By the mirror symmetry noted in s03 (flipping x1 swaps R1↔R2, flipping x2 swaps E1↔E2, both within-class), the max-margin hyperplane cannot tilt toward x1 or x2 at all -- it must be purely w=(0,0,c). Solving so that y·(w·x+b)=1 exactly at the boundary of each class's z-value gives c=1/7.5=0.133333 and b=-1.133333.

Check 4 -- reproducing the table above

JAVASCRIPT · LIFTING AND SCORING THE FOUR POINTS 
const raw = [
  { p: [1, 0], y: -1, name: "R1" }, { p: [-1, 0], y: -1, name: "R2" },
  { p: [0, 4], y: 1, name: "E1" },  { p: [0, -4], y: 1, name: "E2" },
];
const phi = (x) => [x[0], x[1], x[0]*x[0] + x[1]*x[1]];
const w = [0, 0, 1 / 7.5], b = -8.5 / 7.5;
raw.forEach(({ p, y, name }) => {
  const z = phi(p);
  const f = w[0]*z[0] + w[1]*z[1] + w[2]*z[2] + b;
  console.log(name, y * f); // R1: 1, R2: 1, E1: 1, E2: 1
});
12
DIAGNOSIS

Check 5 -- brute-force 3D directional sweep

To confirm w=(0,0,c) is actually the max-margin direction and not just A working direction, 500,000 random unit vectors in 3D were tested as candidate separating directions against the four lifted points. The best margin found was 7.497420, against a hand-derived value of 7.500000 -- a 0.03% gap fully explained by random sampling never landing exactly on the true optimum. The best direction found was (-0.0029, -0.0006, -1.0000) -- effectively the z-axis, matching w's own direction.

13
EVIDENCE + CONTROL

scikit-learn, live -- linear on raw versus linear on mapped

Fitting SVC(kernel='linear') directly on the raw (x1,x2) features predicts all four points as Escalate, 50% accuracy -- confirming Checks 1-3 in a real library, not just hand arithmetic. Fitting the identical linear SVC on the 3-feature mapped data (x1, x2, x1²+x2²) reaches 100% accuracy, names all four points as support vectors (support_ = [0,1,2,3], n_support_ = [2,2]), and reports a fitted margin of 1/||coef_|| = 7.499505 -- matching the hand-derived 7.500000 to four decimal places (the small residual is the SVC's default soft-margin C, not a hard-margin solve).

14
MATHEMATICS

The kernel trick's actual claim

Margin-maximization only ever needs dot products between points, never the points' raw coordinates by themselves. So instead of computing φ(x) and φ(x') and then dotting them, define a function that returns the same number directly from x and x':

K(x, x') = φ(x)·φ(x') = x1x1' + x2x2' + (x1²+x2²)(x1'²+x2'²) = x·x' + ||x||²||x'||²

The right-hand side is exactly the k(x,y) quoted from Wikipedia's Kernel method page in s08. K can be evaluated for any pair of points without ever constructing φ(x) as an actual 3-number vector -- that skipped construction is the entire trick.

CODE

Check 6 -- confirming K(x,x') really equals φ(x)·φ(x')

JAVASCRIPT · EXPLICIT MAP VERSUS DIRECT FORMULA, ALL 16 PAIRS 
const phi = (x) => [x[0], x[1], x[0]*x[0] + x[1]*x[1]];
const dot3 = (a, b) => a[0]*b[0] + a[1]*b[1] + a[2]*b[2];
const K = (x, xp) => x[0]*xp[0] + x[1]*xp[1]
  + (x[0]*x[0]+x[1]*x[1]) * (xp[0]*xp[0]+xp[1]*xp[1]);

const pts = { R1:[1,0], R2:[-1,0], E1:[0,4], E2:[0,-4] };
for (const a in pts) for (const b in pts) {
  const explicit = dot3(phi(pts[a]), phi(pts[b]));
  const direct = K(pts[a], pts[b]);
  console.log(a, b, explicit === direct); // true, all 16 pairs
}

EVIDENCE + CONTROL

scikit-learn, live -- the RBF kernel on the untouched raw data

Fitting SVC(kernel='rbf', gamma=0.05) directly on the original two-feature (x1,x2) data -- no φ, no manual third column -- reaches 100% accuracy and names all four points as support vectors (support_ = [0,1,2,3]). This is the actual kernel trick in action: the RBF kernel computes an implicit, much higher-dimensional similarity between points without this lesson, or scikit-learn, ever writing down what that mapped space looks like.

17
CITATION

scikit-learn's real kernel functions

Quoted directly from scikit-learn's own documentation (scikit-learn, Kernel functions): linear is ⟨x,x'⟩; polynomial is (γ⟨x,x'⟩+r)^d, where d is degree and r is coef0; rbf is exp(-γ||x-x'||²), where γ is gamma; sigmoid is tanh(γ⟨x,x'⟩+r). None of these four is the K(x,x') derived by hand in s14 -- that formula was built specifically for this dataset's own φ, as a teaching device, not as a stand-in for any one of scikit-learn's built-in kernel names.

18
IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

What this example does and does not prove

This lesson's φ and K are hand-built and verified for exactly one 4-point dataset -- they are a worked illustration of the trick's mechanics, not scikit-learn's polynomial kernel (which expands γ⟨x,x'⟩+r to a power, a different and more general construction) and not its RBF kernel either. Separately, it is a well-established and widely cited mathematical fact that the RBF kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature space (Wikipedia, Radial basis function kernel: "The feature space of the kernel has an infinite number of dimensions"). That specific claim is cited here, not independently re-derived -- deriving the RBF kernel's own infinite-dimensional expansion from scratch is out of scope for this concept.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How do you prove a dataset is not linearly separable, rather than just failing to find a line?
Check whether the two classes' convex hulls intersect -- if they do, no separating hyperplane exists, by a standard theorem. This lesson additionally cross-checked that with a brute-force angular sweep and a non-converging Perceptron.
What specifically does adding z=x1²+x2² give a linear classifier that x1 and x2 alone cannot?
It exposes each point's distance from the origin as a feature a straight-line boundary can threshold directly -- something no linear combination of x1 and x2 alone can express.
What is the actual definition of the kernel trick, precisely?
Replacing every dot-product φ(x)·φ(x') that an algorithm needs with a kernel function K(x,x') that returns the same value directly from x and x', without ever constructing φ(x) as an explicit vector.
Is the feature map used in this lesson the same thing as scikit-learn's polynomial kernel?
No. It's a hand-built 3-feature map chosen to fit this one dataset. scikit-learn's actual kernel formulas -- linear, polynomial, rbf, sigmoid -- are different, more general functions, quoted directly in s17.
23
CORRECTION

Common misconception

The kernel trick does not mean "try adding more features until something works."Adding an arbitrary feature does nothing by itself -- z=x1²+x2² works here because it captures exactly the property (distance from center) that actually distinguishes Resolve from Escalate in this dataset. A real kernel function is not a guess; it corresponds to a specific, well-defined feature space (linear, polynomial, or the RBF kernel's infinite-dimensional one), chosen because its similarity structure suits the problem, not applied at random.
