01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Three fixed nonlinear functions, applied to a number you already have

An activation function takes one number -- the pre-activation z = w·x + b -- and squashes or reshapes it into the value the unit actually outputs. Concept 02's perceptron used the simplest possible one: a step function, 1 if z≥0 else 0. This concept covers the three activations that actually show up in real networks instead: sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU. Same z in every case, three different functions on top of it.

First memory: Same twelve z's Concept 02 already produced. The only thing this concept changes is which fixed curve sits on top of each one.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 02 already ran these twelve numbers through a step -- this is the same z's, a different curve

Concept 02's perceptron converged on the module's shared 8-row CFPB table at epoch 10 after 35 updates, landing at w=(68,−10,2), b=11, 8/8 correct. The identical update rule run on the classic 4-point XOR pattern never converged, locking into a cycle at w=(0,1), b=0 that caps at exactly 50% training accuracy. Those two weight vectors, dropped back into the same rows and points, are the only source of every z used in this concept -- nothing here is invented.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Sigmoid, tanh and ReLU, in their actual closed forms

Sigmoid: σ(z) = 1/(1+e−z). Tanh: tanh(z) = (ez−e−z)/(ez+e−z). ReLU: max(0,z). These are exactly what PyTorch ships as nn.Sigmoid, nn.Tanh and nn.ReLU -- not textbook simplifications. Scikit-learn's own MLPClassifier calls the sigmoid option 'logistic' rather than 'sigmoid', which is worth knowing before it looks like a missing option in that library.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Twelve pre-activations, reused rather than reinvented

Eight of the twelve are Concept 02's own CFPB rows, evaluated at its converged weights: z = −19, −51, 65, −319, 33, −453, 53, −285 for rows A through H. The other four are the XOR points, evaluated at Concept 02's stuck weights: z = 0, 1, 1, 0 at (0,0), (1,1), (0,1), (1,0). Every one of these twelve numbers is a real output of a real algorithm run in the previous concept, not a number chosen to make a clean example.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

"This unit's own slope" is not "the network's gradient"

Every derivative computed in this concept -- σ'(z), tanh'(z), ReLU'(z) -- is the local slope of one function at one fixed input. It says nothing yet about how a mistake at the network's output would actually flow backward through several layers to reach this unit; that chain-rule computation is what backpropagation does, and it isn't built until Concept 07. Calling today's numbers "the gradient" would overstate what a single activation's own derivative can tell you.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

"An activation function works the same regardless of how large z gets"

"Sigmoid and tanh behave like they do near z=0 no matter what."

False, and this concept's own numbers show it: at CFPB scale (|z| up to 453), both functions' derivatives round to zero -- 4/12 live for each, and every one of those four live cases is an XOR point, not a CFPB row.

"ReLU has no scale problem, full stop."

Overstated -- ReLU stays alive on any positive z regardless of scale (5/12 here), but it has its own dead zone: any z at or below zero, permanently, with no recovery once a unit lands there.

08 · PROJECT MAPPING

A likely misreading: sigmoid's two tails don't saturate at the same rate

It's tempting to assume "sigmoid saturates at 0 and 1" means both ends behave symmetrically. They don't, in floating point: sigmoid hits an exact, bit-for-bit 1.0 starting around z≈37, but only reaches an exact 0.0 near z≈−745. Every negative CFPB-scale z in this concept (−19 down to −453) is far short of −745, so those rows report a tiny-but-nonzero sigmoid output, not a clean zero -- even though their derivatives are just as dead either way.

05
DEFINITIONS

Sigmoid, defined

σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

This is exactly logistic regression's own squashing function, and exactly what PyTorch ships as torch.nn.Sigmoid (curled directly against its live documentation, a 301 redirect landing at 200 on docs.pytorch.org). Output range is the open interval (0,1) -- never exactly 0 or 1 in principle, though floating point disagrees at the extremes, covered in Section 16.

06
DEFINITIONS

Tanh, defined

tanh(z) = (ez − e−z) / (ez + e−z)

Cited to torch.nn.Tanh, curled live the same way as Sigmoid above. Tanh is sigmoid's own rescaled, zero-centered cousin: the identity tanh(z) = 2·σ(2z) − 1 holds exactly in real arithmetic, and was checked here at z=−19, 0, 1, 33 and 65 -- every difference between the two sides came out at floating-point epsilon (about 2.22×10&supminus;¹⁶) or smaller, never a real disagreement.

07
DEFINITIONS

ReLU, defined

ReLU(z) = max(0, z)

Cited to torch.nn.ReLU, the simplest of the three: identity for positive z, a hard floor of zero otherwise. Scikit-learn's own MLPClassifier(activation=...) parameter accepts exactly four string values -- 'identity', 'logistic', 'tanh', 'relu' -- confirming these are real, currently-shipping options in a library this course has already used, not a hand-picked shortlist.

08
DERIVATION

Sigmoid's derivative, derived by the quotient rule

Write σ(z) = u/v with u=1, v=1+e−z, so u'=0 and v'=−e−z. The quotient rule gives σ'(z) = (u'v − uv')/v² = e−z/(1+e−z)². Splitting that into two factors of 1/(1+e−z) and simplifying the second factor as (1+e−z−1)/(1+e−z) recovers σ(z)·(1−σ(z)) exactly.

σ'(z) = σ(z)·(1 − σ(z))
09
DERIVATION

Tanh's derivative, derived the same way

With u=ez−e−z and v=ez+e−z, note u'=v and v'=u. The quotient rule gives tanh'(z) = (u'v − uv')/v² = (v² − u²)/v² = 1 − (u/v)², and u/v is exactly tanh(z) by definition.

tanh'(z) = 1 − tanh(z)²
10
DERIVATION

ReLU's derivative: a two-branch constant, and a convention at the kink

ReLU'(z) = 1 if z > 0, else 0 if z < 0

At exactly z=0 the function has a corner -- a kink with no single well-defined slope, mathematically. This concept adopts the coded convention z > 0 ? 1 : 0, meaning z=0 itself is treated as dead. That is a software choice, not a mathematical fact; Section 17 flags exactly what changes if a different convention is used instead.

15
DIAGNOSIS

Why sigmoid and tanh go dead specifically at CFPB scale

Concept 02's perceptron trained on eight rows with real-world-scaled features (case ages up to 60 days, several binary flags) and converged to weights that push |z| into the hundreds -- as far as −453. Both sigmoid and tanh have most of their curvature packed into a narrow band around z=0; outside roughly |z|>5 they're already flat to several decimal places, and CFPB's converged z's are one to two orders of magnitude past that. XOR's z's, by contrast, sit at exactly 0 or 1 -- comfortably inside the region where both functions still have real curvature, which is the only reason any of today's twelve points show a live gradient at all.

16
CORRECTION

Sigmoid's saturation is asymmetric in floating point

The two tails do not saturate at the same rate.Sigmoid hits an exact, bit-for-bit 1.0 starting at z≈37, but reaches an exact 0.0 only near z≈−745. None of this concept's negative z's (−19 down to −453) come anywhere near −745, so every one of them reports a tiny-but-nonzero sigmoid output rather than a clean zero -- Row F's 1.84×10&supminus;¹⁹⁷ is small enough to be indistinguishable from zero in any practical sense, but it is not literally zero. Don't let "shows as 0 or 1" simplify into "both tails saturate at the same rate" -- they don't.
17
CORRECTION

ReLU's z=0 kink is a coded convention, not a fact

Two of the four XOR points sit exactly at the kink.XOR points (0,0) and (1,0) both land at z=0, which this concept reports as dead only because of the z > 0 ? 1 : 0 convention chosen in Section 10. A different, equally defensible convention -- treating z=0 as alive with derivative 0.5, or 1 -- would change ReLU's XOR-side tally from 2/4 to 3/4 or 4/4 without changing a single underlying number. The 5/12 headline figure in Section 14 is downstream of this one coded choice, not an inevitability.
18
SCOPE HEDGE

What this derivative is not: the network's gradient

Every derivative in this concept is the local slope of one unit's own transfer function, at one fixed z. It is not the quantity backpropagation actually computes at that unit during training -- which is this local slope multiplied by however the error at every downstream layer flows back to it, via the chain rule. That full computation, and what it means for a unit buried several layers deep, is Concept 07's job. A dead local derivative here is necessary but not sufficient evidence that a real network's training signal would vanish at that unit.

19
MECHANISM

Named forward: this is a concrete instance of vanishing gradients

Eight of twelve local derivatives rounding to zero, at realistic trained-weight scale, is not a coincidence engineered for this lesson -- it is a real, observed instance of the vanishing-gradient problem: a symptom, not yet a diagnosis or a fix. Concept 05, "Weight initialization and the vanishing/exploding gradient problem," is where that problem gets named formally and where the first real mitigation (choosing an initial weight scale that keeps pre-activations away from the saturated region) gets built. Nothing about that mechanism is covered here.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: derive σ'(z) from σ(z)=1/(1+e−z).
Apply the quotient rule to u/v with u=1, v=1+e−z: σ'(z)=e−z/(1+e−z)². Split that as [1/(1+e−z)]·[e−z/(1+e−z)], and rewrite the second bracket as (1+e−z−1)/(1+e−z) = 1−σ(z). The result is σ(z)·(1−σ(z)).
Conceptual: why does ReLU avoid two-sided saturation but still suffer from dead units?
ReLU's derivative is a constant 1 for every positive z, no matter how large -- there's no upper saturation region at all, unlike sigmoid or tanh. But for any z at or below zero its derivative is a constant 0, and nothing about training moves a unit's own pre-activation independently of its weights -- once a unit's weights push it into that region for all its inputs, it has no gradient left to escape with. Avoiding one failure mode doesn't mean avoiding all of them.
Practical: what would you check in a real network showing this same all-CFPB-rows-dead pattern?
First, the actual scale of the pre-activations feeding each activation -- this concept's own numbers show the problem is the weight scale, not the activation choice in isolation. Then whether initialization or a learning-rate schedule is pushing weights into that saturated region early in training, which is exactly what Concept 05 covers next; and whether switching to ReLU (or a variant) changes the pattern, keeping in mind ReLU has its own one-sided failure mode from Section 20.
