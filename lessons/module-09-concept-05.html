MODULE 09 · CONCEPT 05

Before a network learns anything,
its starting scale can already break it.

A trained network's weights end up somewhere -- Concept 04 showed what happens when they end up saturated. This concept goes earlier: to the very first forward pass, before any training step exists, and asks what happens if the *initial* draw is wrong in scale. Zero collapses by symmetry. Too large explodes. Too small vanishes. Xavier and He are the two fan-size-aware fixes, and this concept runs all five, for real, on this module's own standardized CFPB table.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

Starting weights aren't neutral -- their scale can decide the outcome before training begins

Concept 04 showed dead gradients in a trained network. This concept goes back to before training exists at all, to the very first forward pass, and isolates one single ingredient: how big or small the initial weight draw is.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Before a network is trained, its weights already have some starting values

If those starting values are wrong in scale -- not wrong in "direction," just too big or too small -- the network can be broken before a single training step happens. This concept is about that starting scale.

02 · DEFINITION

Vanishing and exploding gradients, defined by one product

As an error signal moves backward through many layers, the gradient reaching an early layer is a product of one factor per layer (roughly: that layer's weights times its activation's local derivative). If those per-layer factors are consistently smaller than 1, the product shrinks toward zero with depth -- the vanishing gradient problem. If consistently larger than 1, the product grows without bound -- the exploding gradient problem. This concept isolates one ingredient of that product -- initial weight scale, checked at the very first forward pass, before training or depth are involved.

First memory: vanishing and exploding are two ends of the same product, not two unrelated bugs -- and this concept only ever looks at one layer, at initialization, not the full multi-layer product depth would produce.

04 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 04 showed a symptom. This concept explains where it can come from before training even starts

Concept 04 showed a symptom (a trained network with dead gradients) without explaining why weights ever land in the saturated zone in the first place. Before any training happens, the very first forward pass already has real numbers flowing through it, and those numbers depend entirely on how the weights were drawn. Get the scale wrong and the network is dead or degenerate on pass one -- training can't fix what never had a gradient to work with.

05 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

The simplest broken scheme: set every weight to exactly zero

Run any of the 8 CFPB rows through [3,4,1] this way (node-verified, Section 07): every one of the 4 hidden pre-activations comes out 0, for every single row, no matter which row you pick -- var(z1) = 0 across all 32 values. Every hidden unit is computing the identical thing, forever, because they started identical and nothing about the update rule breaks that symmetry. This is why "just start at zero" fails -- not because zero is small, but because it's symmetric.

06 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Xavier and He: two real formulas, each targeting a different activation, both fan-size-aware

Xavier/Glorot (for sigmoid/tanh) draws each weight with Var(W) = 2 / (fan_in + fan_out); He/Kaiming (for ReLU) draws each weight with Var(W) = 2 / fan_in -- a larger variance, because ReLU zeroes out roughly half its inputs and needs the surviving half to carry more variance to compensate. Both are built to keep the variance of the pre-activations roughly stable from one layer to the next, instead of shrinking (vanishing) or growing (exploding) as it passes through.

07 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

Same [3,4,1] flow as Concept 06 -- with real starting weights instead of ad hoc ones

Standardized xVar(x)=1, Section 06
z1 = W1·x + b1 → activation → a1var(z1) tracked per scheme
z2 = W2·a1 + b2var(z2) tracked per scheme

Standardized x → z1 = W1·x + b1 → activation → a1 → z2 = W2·a1 + b2 → raw output. The new thing this concept tracks is the ratio var(z2) / var(z1) -- how much the signal's spread grew or shrank crossing one layer. Node-verified (Section 11/09): that ratio is 0.3775 at Xavier scale (mild shrink, healthy), 1.78 at 10×-too-large scale (growing), and effectively near-zero at 0.01×-too-small scale (collapsing) -- the same one-layer step, three different weight scales.

08 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Xavier is not He, and initialization-time is not training-time

Xavier and He aren't interchangeable.

Xavier assumes an activation like tanh that's roughly linear near 0; He is built specifically to offset ReLU killing about half its inputs.

This is an initialization-time effect, not Concept 04's training-time effect.

Concept 04's dead units came from converged weights after training; this concept's dead/collapsed units happen on the very first forward pass, before any learning.

Vanishing and exploding are one slider, not two bugs.

The naive-small and naive-large schemes in Section 08/09 are literally the same architecture with only the weight scale changed.

09 · COMMON MISTAKES

"Small weights are always the safe choice"

All-zero initialization.

Symmetry never breaks -- see b05.

One fixed standard deviation for every layer, ignoring fan_in/fan_out.

Section 08/09: std=10 kills 29/32 tanh units; std=0.01 shrinks the output layer's variance by over 4,000×.

Using Xavier on a ReLU network, or He on a tanh network.

The variance target is derived for a specific activation's shape -- swapping them defeats the derivation.

Forgetting to standardize inputs before applying either formula.

Both derivations assume Var(x) = 1; skip that step and even a correctly-scaled Xavier/He draw won't behave as advertised.

11 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

Why does all-zero init fail even though 0 isn't a "big" number?

It's not a scale failure -- every hidden unit starts identical and stays identical, because nothing about the update rule breaks that symmetry (b05).

Why does He use a larger variance than Xavier, not a smaller one?

ReLU zeroes out roughly half its inputs, so the surviving half needs more variance to compensate -- Var(W)=2/fan_in versus Xavier's 2/(fan_in+fan_out) (b06).

Why does standardizing the inputs matter for either formula to work as derived?

Both Xavier and He are derived assuming Var(x)=1 -- skip standardization and the "principled" variance target no longer matches what the derivation promised (b09).

Teach-back questions

  1. Why is "vanishing" and "exploding" better described as one slider than two separate bugs?
  2. What would you expect to change, and what would stay exactly the same, if you re-ran the He scheme with unstandardized CFPB features instead of standardized ones?
  3. Concept 04 found dead gradients in a trained network. This concept finds dead/collapsed units before training starts. What's the actual difference between those two findings?

05
MATHEMATICS

Vanishing and exploding gradients, named formally

For an L-layer network, the gradient reaching layer 1 is (roughly) a product of L per-layer factors,
each roughly (that layer's weight scale) × (that layer's activation's local derivative).
All factors consistently < 1 → the product shrinks toward 0 as L grows: vanishing.
All factors consistently > 1 → the product grows without bound: exploding.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: derive Xavier's variance target and explain what it's trying to preserve.
Var(W) = 2/(fan_in+fan_out). It's trying to keep the variance of the pre-activations roughly equal going into and coming out of a layer, so that neither vanishing nor exploding sets in purely from layer-to-layer scale drift -- assuming an activation like tanh that's roughly linear near 0.
Conceptual: why is He's variance target different from Xavier's, and by how much?
He uses Var(W)=2/fan_in -- exactly double Xavier's 2/(fan_in+fan_out) when fan_in=fan_out, per Sections 10 and 12's own numbers (0.6667 vs 0.2857 for layer 1). ReLU zeroes out roughly half its inputs, so the surviving half needs more variance to keep the layer's overall signal from shrinking.
Practical: what would "amber" mean on this concept's own scale, and why does standardization avoid it?
"Amber" would mean a scheme that's alive on some but not all 32 checked units -- a partial, scale-driven degradation rather than the clean collapse of zero init or the near-full aliveness of a correctly-scaled scheme. A superseded earlier pass found Xavier landing there specifically because it skipped standardizing the inputs first; once Var(x)=1 is enforced (Section 06), Xavier reaches the full 32/32 this concept reports in Section 11.
